Chapter I

The Duke's Throne

883 – 884
In the hand of Chrysogonos, with MatakoA Roman Greek of the company, a soldier since he was fifteen, who took the log from Matako in the third month of 883 when the trade changed from guarding roads to making dukes. He writes the work as work; Matako keeps two pages of her own, marked as hers.

The book changes hands

My name is Chrysogonos and I am thirty-three years old, and until the third month of this year I had never written anything longer than a requisition. I am writing this because the Captain asked me to, in the camp west of Constantinople in the last week of the revelry, and because Matako put the book into my hands and went back to her anvil without arguing, and because in this company a thing that has been decided is not argued with twice.

I am a Roman Greek. I was born inside the Empire, I have been a soldier since I was fifteen, and it is eighteen years this spring since I first took pay. I have served nine companies, two fleets and one siege that never ended, and I came to this one at Constantinople in the ninth month of 879 with four other Greeks and no expectations of any kind, because a company that has just come out of a war is either good or finished and you can tell which inside a month.

Matako kept the last book, and she kept it the way a smith keeps a thing: what was made, what it cost, who was hurt, what the weather did, and whether the iron took the temper. I have read all of it twice and the last chapter three times, and I am not going to better it, and I am not going to imitate it either. A company is not one story. It is a workshop and a market and a court of law and a machine for killing, and the person who keeps the book decides which of those the company is going to be remembered as.

She kept it as a workshop, because she is the workshop. I am keeping this one as a machine, because that is what I am. It is not a better book. It is the book the years need, from the twelfth month of 883 onward, since those years are about the thing the machine is for.

Two things about how I mean to do it. The first is that I will write what I saw, and where I did not see a thing I will write what the roll says, and where the roll is silent I will say so and leave it. That will not make this book short.

The second is that Matako has not finished with it. She has kept the company's iron for thirteen years and she will keep two pages of every chapter I write as long as she is in a condition to hold a pen, and I have promised her the pages are hers, unedited and marked as hers, and I will keep that promise; so when you come to a page headed with her name, it is her voice and her arithmetic and not mine, and if it disagrees with mine, believe her, because she was there longer.

I asked her why she gave it up in the third month when there was no reason to give it up before the autumn. She said: because in the autumn we stopped being paid to keep things and started being paid to change them, and I do not know how to write that, and you do.

I have thought about that sentence for a year. She was right, and she was also being kind, which is not a thing she does often and which I did not deserve on the day.

What the company was in the third month of 883

I will put the machine on the table before I drive it, because a reader who does not know what a company is will read the rest of this book as a series of accidents.

On the first day of the third month of 883 the Distinguished Host had twenty companies of soldiers of five kinds. Two of them were armoured foot, which is to say men in mail with a spear and a shield who can stand still while something is done to them. One was Armenian horse, which the roll calls the Ayrudzi, and which is the heaviest thing in the company and the only thing in it that is meant to be spent all at once. One was light horse, outriders and eyes and the men who find the water. And the last was Zhanmadao, which is a Chinese word for men with two-handed swords who are put where a horse is expected to come.

There were thirteen hundred and forty-five men present and sixteen hundred on the roll. That difference is not theft, or not only theft. A hundred on the roll is a company's paper strength; eighty in the field is a company. A company of a hundred can put seventy files in a line with their officers behind them. Anyone who tells you his company is a hundred strong is telling you about his paperwork, and every lord in the world knows it, and every lord in the world pays for the paper anyway, because the alternative is counting.

Twenty companies is two thousand on the roll and it is about twelve hundred men in a line on a good morning, and twelve hundred men in a line is three quarters of a mile from wing to wing if you do not double them, which is why no one fights in a single line. That is the whole of my trade in one sentence: a battle is not men, it is frontage, and the man who understands that can beat a bigger army than his with a company that has been paid on time.

The Captain commands this machine, and I am going to write about him the way a man writes about weather: what he did, where he was, what it cost. I have known him five years. He is thirty-three, he is a Hellene, he holds no land and he has never held any, and he can put more men in a field than most counts in the Balkan country, and he pays them on a day nobody has to ask about. I have served lords who were braver and lords who were better company. I have never served anyone who could look at a valley and tell you where the water would be at noon.

The company went out of the camp west of Constantinople in the third month, north toward the Danube, because there was a contract waiting at Silistra and the contract was for the road, and the road was where the money was. That is the whole reason for the first half of this year, and I want it written plainly, because the men have since decided that the Captain turned toward Macedon out of destiny and there was no destiny in it. There was a road, and a fee, and a lord who had written a letter.

Silistra

Silistra is a river town and a garrison and a place where three roads meet, and it is the northern edge of the world that the company had been working in since Severin. We were there in the last days of the third month and the whole of the fourth.

The work was dull and it was well paid. We held the crossing, we escorted the salt and the cloth, we sat in a fort that was already ours by contract and looked north across the water at a country none of us had any business in. In six weeks we lost one man, to a fever, and eleven to a fight in the town that started over a horse and ended with a knife in a boy's arm and cost us four days of pay in fines.

The Captain went out most mornings to the crossing and did nothing there. I asked him once what he was looking at and he said: the ferry. I said, the ferry. And he said: what it charges, who owns it, how many cross, and what happens to the price when an army is on this bank. Then he said: a river crossing is worth more than a battle to whoever owns it, and a battle is worth nothing at all if the loser can cross.

That is what I mean about the machine. He was not looking at a river. He was looking at a price.

Tokifusa read the roll on the last day of the fourth month, in the yard of the fort, in front of everybody, which is a thing the company does at the end of a contract and which I had never seen another company do. He reads the living first, in order, and then he reads the dead of the year, with the month and the place, and nobody talks and nobody leaves. It took a quarter of an hour. There was one name on the back. A fever at Silistra is not a glorious death and I have seen men die worse for less, but the roll does not grade, and that is the point of the roll.

Drăgășani

We went west in the fifth month and the country began to rise. At Drăgășani a man called Petre came in and took the oath, and I am going to give him two paragraphs because of what happened later, and because of a joke that ran through the camp for a year.

Petre was forty-four years old, out of the Bulgarian march, and he had been a sergeant in somebody else's company for eleven years and had never been given a company of his own, and I could tell why inside a week: he was better at the work than the men above him and had said so. He could handle a mule train better than anyone I have served with, including me. He had a wife in the hills and no money and a way of speaking to the youngest men in the company that made them better and made them hate him for a month.

There were two Petres in that war, and the other one was twenty years old and fighting on the other side, and neither of them ever met the other, and the men thought this was the funniest thing that had ever happened. It was not funny at Câmpulung. But it was funny at Drăgășani in the fifth month, when the roll had to be read out with the man's birthplace attached to his name the way a slave's is, and the company laughed for three days.

The Captain took him because the company had two mule trains and one man who could handle them, and because in a bad winter a company that cannot move its own bread is a company that will be eating its own horses by the second month. He did not take him because he liked him. I asked Petre afterwards whether it was strange to be taken for a thing and not for himself, and he said it was the only honest kind of hiring he had ever had.

At Breznik in the sixth month we took a small contract and did it in nine days and lost nobody. The hills were getting higher and there was iron in the villages and the smiths there were better than Matako expected and worse than they thought. I remember the sixth month mostly as seven hundred men going uphill in the rain and the Captain riding alongside the forge cart talking to Matako about charcoal, which is the sort of thing that never appears in a chronicle and occupies nine days of a life.

Kičevo and Debar

Then the road turned south into the Macedonian country, and we were in it from the sixth month to the eighth, at Kičevo and at Debar, and I am going to take my time here, because this is the part of the year that nobody in the company understood at the time and everybody understood later.

The country is hills with a lake in a hole in them, and two old roads crossing, and it is high enough that the summer is a month late and the hay is cut in the eighth month. There are orchards and herds and a market at Debar that sells wool and hides and a black wine that four of our men got drunk on in a way that took them two days to recover from. There is paving under the grass in places, from when this was a province of something older than any of the kingdoms now arguing over it, and in the sixth month the rain washes the soil off it and for a week you can see the road.

I had been in this country before. I had marched through it at nineteen in somebody else's company and I remembered the smell of the lake and nothing else. What was different was the size of the thing behind us. When I was nineteen I came through with two hundred men and we were a nuisance. In the eighth month of 883 a thousand three hundred men came down that road with a forge cart and a chest and a standard, and the market at Debar asked the Captain's price for wool before it asked anybody else's.

The Captain did a thing at Kičevo that I did not understand for a year and have thought about since. He went up alone, on the third evening, to a height above the town where you can see both roads at once, and he sat there until it was too dark to see, and came down and said nothing about it. I know he did it because I followed him, at three hundred paces, which was my job then and is my job now.

He was not praying. I have watched him pray and it does not look like that. He was not weeping either, and there is a story in the company that he wept on that hill, and the story is a lie. He was looking down at a valley with two roads in it and working out, in the way he works out a ferry, where an army could stand and where it could not, and what a man who held that height would be able to charge for the use of it.

I have known men who wanted a country. They want it the way a man wants a woman, with a heat in them, and they say the name of it too much. This one did not say the name of it once in eight weeks. He learned the price of the wool and the depth of the lake and the name of every village headman between Kičevo and Debar, and he paid a boy four pence to draw him the two roads on a board, and he put the board in the chest with the gods.

I am not going to write that he saw a kingdom in that valley. He did not say so, and a man's silence is not evidence, and I have watched this company for five years and I have learned the difference between a thing I saw and a thing I have decided about afterwards.

But I will write this, because I saw it. In the eighth month, on the last night before we went north again, he asked me a question about the ground at the crossing below Debar and then, when I had answered it, he asked another question that had nothing to do with the ground. He asked what the villages paid in tax and to whom, and when I said I did not know, he said: find out, and do not let anybody in the company ask why you are asking.

I found out. It took eleven days and two bribes and I wrote it on four sides of paper. It is in the chest now, with the board and the gods and a dead man's leaves. Whatever else this year was, that was the month it stopped being a contract.

Vratsa, and the wound

We were north again in the ninth month, at Vratsa, which is a town under a crag with a river on one side of it and a road going up through a gate that a hundred men could hold against a thousand and no one has ever bothered to. We did nine days of work there and collected a fee that was three weeks late and had been promised in silver and was paid in salt, which the paymaster accepted, because salt at Vratsa in the ninth month is money.

Then east and north into the Székely country, and there, in the second week of the tenth month, the Captain was wounded, and I am going to write this the way I would write it in a report, which is to say exactly as far as the evidence goes.

He went out at dusk with three men to look at a ford. He came back after dark on foot, holding his side, with his coat cut through at the left and the three men behind him saying nothing. He walked into the light, sat down on the anvil cart, and said: Matako. That is the whole of what he said.

Matako cut the coat off him and put four stitches in his side and one in his forearm, and by the eleventh hour he was giving orders about the morning's march in a voice that had a hole in it. He did not send for me. He sent for Tokifusa and the two officers of the armoured foot, and the orders were about the road, and none of them was about what had happened at the ford.

I have been a soldier for eighteen years and I know what a wound looks like when it comes from the front in a fight. This was in the left side under the ribs, going in and down, a hand's width below the armpit, which is where a knife goes when a man who knows where to put it is very close and the other man has turned.

I asked the three men. One of them was Petre, who is not a coward and not a liar, and he said: the Captain told us to wait at the ford and he went up the bank alone. I said, and? And he said, and then he came back. And I said, was there a man up there? And Petre looked at me for a while and said: I did not see one.

I have written this out at this length because the record has one word for the tenth month of 883 and the word is wounded, and because the company spent the next two years explaining it to itself and every explanation was worse than the last. The version the men settled on by the spring was that a Vlach had done it for a reason. Another version, which I heard once and only once, from a man who had had wine, was that the Captain did it himself for reasons of his own. I do not believe either of them. I believe three men went to a ford with him and came back without whatever it was they saw, and that this company is full of men who can keep a thing shut when they decide to.

He was on a horse inside four days and giving the orders from it, and he was not right until the twelfth month, and he never once mentioned the ford to me in the five years I have known him, and I have stopped asking, and I have not stopped noticing.

The duke's war

On the thirteenth day of the tenth month of 883 the company took a contract with Duke Tepes, who held Muntenia, and the contract was to save his throne, and it was the first war this company ever fought for somebody else's crown.

Muntenia is the country north of the Danube where the river comes out of the hills; there is good pasture in it and no silver and a great many lords who have been arguing for two hundred years about who is the lord of the ones who are arguing. Tepes was the duke. A man called Valentin had raised the country against him, and had with him Petre of the other name, who was twenty years old and had a following, and Iuga, who had more men than either.

For Tepes there was the company, and the younger Gavril — who was thirty-five and had a claim of his own on something and had decided, that autumn, that Tepes's enemies were his enemies — and Corneliu, and a Turk called Suleyman who came with four hundred horse and a reputation and left with the same four hundred and a better one.

That is what the war looked like on paper in the tenth month: a duke with a hired company and three allies who each wanted something, against a rebellion with two lords and the countryside. Nobody in that country thought we would win it. I do not say that as modesty; I say it because they told us so, in the market at Giurgiu, to our faces, and then charged us more for the bread.

We spent the eleventh and twelfth months doing the work that no chronicle ever writes down. We garrisoned four crossings and a mill. We sat in a wet field for eleven days waiting for an army that did not come. We escorted the duke's tax from three villages and were shot at twice from a wood and lost a mule and a boy of nineteen whose name I am not going to look up tonight. We bought barley at a price that went up every week because we were the reason it went up. And we drilled, in the mud, twice a day, when there was no enemy within thirty miles, which is the single most unpopular thing a captain can do to a company in the winter and the single reason the company was still alive in the spring.

Drill is where a company is made. Any fool can be brave at the right moment; the trick is that on the day, twelve hundred men who have never met each other have to do the same thing at the same time without being told, because the telling takes longer than the doing. We drilled the change of front in the mud of Muntenia in the first month of 884 until the armoured foot could do it in the dark, and the men cursed the Captain in four languages and did it, and it is the reason I am writing this chapter instead of a list of the dead.

Matako's page

This page is hers and I have not touched it.

I am not going to write about the war because Chrysogonos will do it and he will do it better and he counts things the way other men sing. I am going to write about the forge, because in the winter of 883 and 884 the forge is where the company lived and nobody has ever put it in a book.

A company of thirteen hundred men in winter needs, per week: four horses shod and eleven replaced, forty spearheads repaired, one cart wheel, two hundred nails, and a man who can put an edge back on a sword that has been used to pry open a door. We had one forge cart and two anvils and me and a boy called Boril, who is forty-three and cannot read and can make a nail out of a rod in the time it takes me to find my hammer.

Charcoal is the whole of it. Not the iron: the iron is easy, the iron is bought. Charcoal in a wet country in the first month is worth more than silver and it is why the company paid what it paid to a charcoal burner outside Giurgiu who cheated us on weight and was the only man in that country we never once threatened.

The Captain was in the forge a great deal that winter, which was not normal and which I understood. He could not ride out on the wound, so he did the one thing in the company that involved standing still and being useful and being warm. He held the tongs. Two hundred times, maybe. He did not talk. He is the only officer I have ever served who understands that a man holding the tongs while somebody else hits the iron is not doing nothing, and if he had been a bad captain, that would still have been true.

On the wound, this much and no more, because I sewed it. It was a knife, and it went in from behind and to the left, and no man in this company carries a knife that makes that shape of hole, and I have seen every knife in this company because I have repaired most of them. I asked him nothing. I have sewn this man up four times since 879 and this was the first time he watched me do it and said nothing at all, not even the joke about my needle that he always makes, and I have decided to leave it there, and I have left it there for two years.

Câmpulung

The war was decided on the twenty-second day of the second month of 884, at a place called Câmpulung, and I am going to write the whole day down, because I was in the middle of it, and because there are seven men in this company who are not, and the day belongs to them too.

By the middle of the second month Valentin's rebellion had a field army and we had a duke who could not leave his own hall and an army that had spent a winter eating barley we had bought for it. Iuga had fourteen hundred and thirty-eight men. I had counted them myself, twice, from a hill, and the second time was because the Captain did not believe the first time, and the difference between my two counts was nineteen and I have never found out where the nineteen went.

We had fifteen hundred and ten. I want the reader to look at those numbers, because they are the reason for everything that follows and they are the reason this chapter is not a victory story. Fifteen hundred against fourteen hundred is not a battle. It is a coin.

On the nineteenth we knew where Iuga was and we went to him, and the Captain rode the last twelve miles in front of the column with the light horse out three miles wide, and at the sixth hour on the twentieth he stood on the ground he was going to fight on and did not say anything for a long time.

The ground was a shallow valley with the river behind it, running north to south, and the water was high with the thaw and there was one ford in it within four miles. Iuga had camped on the east bank and had his back to the water, and I do not know to this day whether he did that because the pasture was good or because he had never once in his life asked where the water would be if he lost.

Here is the plan, and it is worth writing down because it is not clever and it is the sort of thing that only works if the men have been drilled in the mud in the first month. We would refuse the left — that is, we would stand our left back instead of level, so that the enemy's right would have to come forward and inward to reach it. We would put the armoured foot in the centre and let them be hit and not move. We would hold the Zhanmadao behind the centre, out of sight, which is not where the manual says to put them. And when the enemy's horse had committed to the refused flank, the Ayrudzi would come out of the dead ground on our right and go into the flank of a line that was already leaning forward.

It is a hammer and an anvil and a decision. The anvil is the armoured foot, which cannot win anything and can only refuse to lose. The hammer is the heaviest horse we have, spent once. The decision is whether the enemy's horse commits before the hammer is in the air.

The men were told all of it on the evening of the twenty-first, which is the other thing that only this company does. In most companies the plan is a secret kept by officers in a tent from the men who will die of it. The Captain stood on a cart and told fifteen hundred men what he was going to do and what would happen to them if it failed, and then he said: if the left gives, we all die in the water. And then he said: it will not give. And then Tokifusa read out the watches and the men went and mended their boots.

We crossed before dawn on the twenty-second, upstream at the ford, in the dark, without a fire and without a light, fifteen hundred men and eleven carts and one forge cart that Matako would not leave behind and that the Captain was wise enough not to argue about. It took four hours. At the second hour of the morning we were in the position and the men lay down behind their shields and some of them slept, which is the one thing I have never been able to do.

The enemy came on at the third hour, and they came on well. I want to be honest about that, because a man who says his enemy came on badly is usually lying about his own morning. They came forward in three bodies with their horse on the right of their line, which is where it should be, and their centre of pike and wattle, and a reserve of two hundred that Iuga kept behind the camp, and that reserve is the only mistake he made.

The refused left worked for two hours. That is the whole truth of the morning: it worked, and it held, and it did nothing at all. The enemy's right came forward and inward and got a line of armoured foot in the face and stopped, and then for two hours nothing happened that a man could describe, which is what an infantry battle is: two walls of men leaning on their shields and a great deal of noise and a man being dragged out at every hundred paces and his place closing.

I was in the second company of armoured foot, on the right of the centre, and the whole of my morning was a spear that broke twice and a man named Shiina shouting something at me I did not understand and the knowledge, which arrives at about the fourth hour and never leaves you, that if we give two paces we give all of it.

At the fifth hour the enemy's horse committed. They came into the refused flank, into the gap that our left had deliberately opened, and they came in fast and in good order and it was, for about eleven minutes, the worst thing I have ever seen. Then the left did not give. That is all. There is no art in that sentence and it is the sentence the day turned on: seven hundred men took a charge meant to break them and moved back four paces and did not open.

And then the Ayrudzi came out of the dead ground on our right, eight hundred horse at the gallop in a line a quarter of a mile wide, into the flank of an enemy horse that had already been stopped. The noise changed at once; I heard it before I saw it, and I have never heard the same noise since. The Captain had put the horse there at the second hour of the morning and had left it standing for six hours doing nothing, which is harder on horsemen than a fight, and the whole of those six hours was the price of the eleven minutes.

The Zhanmadao came out from behind our centre and went into the pike and wattle with two-handed swords, and the swords do not break a line, they break one man at a time and very suddenly, and a man in a pike line who has seen a sword take the head off the man beside him does not stand there. Then the armoured foot were told to advance, and they advanced with a spear wall into a stopped enemy, and the enemy's centre broke at about the seventh hour, before their horse had finished coming apart.

What happens then is not a battle and there is no honest word for it in Greek or in Latin and the soldiers have a word that I will not put in this book. An army with a river behind it and one ford four miles away does not run away. It goes to the water. Some of them swam, in the second month, in a channel that is a great deal wider than it looks on a sunny afternoon, and most of them did not.

I have counted the counters after accounts in my life and it is not the same thing. One thousand four hundred and thirty-eight men were in that enemy army in the morning. We counted four hundred and eleven bodies on the field and the clerks went out to the ford over three days and took the number of the rest out of the water, and the men who did not leave anything in the water went home and are not counted anywhere.

Seven of ours were killed. Seven, out of fifteen hundred, in a day when we destroyed an army. I have written that number three times on this page and it is the number that every man in this company knows by heart, and it is not a proud number. It is a number that makes men silent in a tent at night, and any soldier reading this will understand why without being told.

Their names are in the roll and I am not going to put them here, because Tokifusa read them out at Giurgiu in the fourth month, in the yard, the way he always does, and there is a thing about hearing a name read out that no page can do.

After the field

The revolt did not end at Câmpulung, but it was ended, and everybody on both sides knew it before the bodies were counted. Valentin's country had put its whole field army into that valley and had a river behind it, and by the last week of the third month there was no rebellion left to fight, and by the fourth month Duke Tepes was sitting in his own hall with the country quiet behind him and the contract paid.

Tepes paid well and paid on time and gave us two barrels of a black wine from his own cellar and a letter to anybody in the world he had ever written to, and the letter was worth more than the wine. I have that letter copied into the book because a company's references are its capital, and because the Captain asked me to copy it in full and then read it back to him twice.

He came out to the camp on the last day of the fourth month and gave a small speech that the company remembered for two years, chiefly because he said that we were the only thing in that country that had done exactly what it said it would do. The Captain answered him in four sentences. I have written them down and they are not interesting and that is the point of them: a man who has just destroyed an army should say four sentences and go back to his tent.

Then the count, which is the part of this trade nobody outside it believes: within a fortnight of Câmpulung there were men coming to the camp to be taken on. Mihai, Vlad, Iancu and Vazul, four Vlachs out of that country who had watched us win and wanted to be on the side that won, and Alajos, who is thirty-seven and had been in three armies and had a way of standing that made officers look twice, and Alexandra, who came in with a bow and a boy's clothes and stayed, and Laurentios and Phyllis, who had come in the winter before and who I will get to, because Laurentios is the reason for the last part of this chapter.

A company fills up after a victory and empties after a defeat, and every man who joins after a victory is worth about half a man who joined after a defeat, and we all know it, and we take them anyway, because the alternative is a company that only ever loses men.

By the fifth month we had five companies of each kind instead of four, which is a hundred companies' worth of paper and twenty-five of soldiers, and the Captain did not take the credit for it in the roll and did not have to. A company does not grow because a captain wishes it. It grows because a lord tells another lord that the company did what it said it would do, and the other lord sends a letter. That is the whole of reputation, and it is the only currency in this trade that cannot be spent by somebody else.

The end of a joke

In the sixth month of 884 the war in the east ended, and I am putting it here because it started in the fourth chapter of this book and because the paymaster had a wager on it.

Mesud Longshanks went to war with Kos-Buwak in the year 876 and it took him eight years, and in the sixth month of 884 it ended and he had not taken Kos-Buwak. Eight years, an army, a fortune, and a claim; and the thing he had gone out to take was still standing at the end of it, with somebody else's flag on it, in a country more than a thousand miles east of anywhere.

The paymaster collected eleven pence and bought the company a barrel, and the toast he gave was: to the wars that nobody wins, and God bless every one of them, for they pay best. That is the end of the joke. It is not a comfortable thing to write in the same chapter as Câmpulung, and I have thought about it a great deal: we destroyed an army for seven men in a February, and a man a thousand miles east of us spent eight years and a fortune to not take a town. The difference between us was not virtue. It was that our enemy was in a valley and had a river behind him.

The wolf

In the ninth month of 884 the company was at Giurgiu and in the country round it, and the work was hunting, and that is not a figure of speech. There were wolves in that country that year in numbers nobody could account for. They had come down out of the hills, or the game had gone, or a hard winter had thinned them and a soft summer had fed them; the hunters said three different things and I have no way to choose between them.

A wolf in a country with winter coming will take a sheep out of a pen and a dog off a doorstep, and it will take a child at the edge of a wood if the child is alone, and once a wolf has done that the village will pay for pelts. So a company of twenty-five hundred men with nothing to do in the ninth month was hired by four villages and a monastery to do what the hunters could not do alone, and we did it well and I am not going to make it into anything but what it was: a great deal of walking, a few dozen wolves, and men who were glad to be doing something with their hands that did not involve a spear wall.

Laurentios came in the winter before with Phyllis, out of the hills above Anchialos, and he had been a hunter of wolves for the lords there for eleven years and paid by the pelt. He was the first man in this company who was an expert in something the company had never needed, and the Captain did a thing with him that I had not seen before: he put himself in the man's hands as a pupil.

In the tenth month Laurentios took Kasame and the Captain out three days running and taught them what he knew. Not hunting; that is the thing that surprised me. He taught them how a wolf thinks. What it will do if you run, and what it will do if you stand, and what it will do if you turn your back on it, and the difference between a wolf that is hunting you and a wolf that has been surprised and is deciding whether it is frightened.

Kasame is forty-five and an Emishi and has been in this company since the northern years, and she took to it like a duck to water and by the third day was arguing with Laurentios about the wind. The Captain took to it the way he takes to everything: he asked questions, wrote nothing down, and got it exactly right in the drill and wrong in the field, which is the difference between understanding a thing and having done it.

On the twenty-eighth day of the tenth month he was caught alone by one.

I was not there. I want that written down plainly, because this is the one page of this chapter where I am not a witness, and I have thought about it for a year and I am going to give the reader exactly what I have, which is the men's account and the record and nothing else.

He had gone out in the afternoon with two men of the light horse, six miles from the camp, to look at a wolf's earth that had been reported by a shepherd. At some point he sent them back, one of them with a lame horse; that is the account and it is the part I believe least, and I have known this man five years and he does send men back.

An hour later and half a mile from where he sent them, in the open, with a fallow field on one side and a scrub of thorn on the other, the wolf came out of the thorn and stopped and looked at him, and he was on foot and had a spear and a knife and a lame hand from the wound in his side that was still not right, and by the account of the men who got there afterwards, which is not the same thing as an account of the meeting, he did not run and did not shout and did not throw.

He stood still and looked at the wolf, and the wolf looked at him, and it went on for a length of time that nobody can agree about and that two men put at a hundred paces and one at a quarter of an hour, and then the wolf turned and went back into the thorn, and he walked to the road and waited for his men.

That is all there is. He was not bitten. He did not kill it. There is no pelt and no story of a fight and no wound out of it, and the men would have had a pelt if there had been a pelt, because this company has never in its history failed to bring a thing home that could be sold.

What the company made of it, before the week was out, was an omen. There is no arguing with a company about omens. The armoured foot decided the wolf was the old duke's war, which had turned away; the Vlachs decided it was the devil of that country and that the Captain had stared it down; the Greeks decided it was nothing because they are all Christians and do not believe in omens until about the second bottle; and one man, whose name I have, decided it was a sign that the Captain had made a bargain at a ford the year before and had now paid something on it.

That last one I heard in a tent and he was very drunk and he has denied it since and he is now an officer. I am not naming him. I am writing down that the company had, by the end of the eleventh month of 884, a story about its captain that has a wolf in it and a bargain in it and a wound that nobody can explain, and that the story was told in my hearing by men who would have died for him and did not entirely like being in the same field.

I am not a Hellene and I have no opinions about what he is. I am a soldier and I have opinions about what he does. I have served under him in a winter of drill, in a battle that lasted nine hours, in a sheep country full of wolves, and I will write this much and stop: he does not lose men, and after a while a company notices that, and the noticing is not entirely comfortable, because men want their captain to be good rather than lucky. Luck is not a thing you can plan for on a wet morning. It is a thing that happens to you.

The count of 884

By the twelfth month he was healed, and the company went into its winter quarters in good order and out of danger for the first time in eighteen months, and I will do what Matako did at the end of her volume and give the count, because a count is the only honest ending to a year.

Twenty-five companies of five kinds. Six hundred horses and eleven carts and one forge cart and one chest. Thirty-nine companions on the roll of the household, which is more than twice what it was when I joined it in 879, and I know most of their names and could shoe rather fewer of their horses than I would like.

He holds no land. That has not changed and it is going to be the thing this book is about. He is thirty-four years old and he can put two thousand men in the field and he owns a cart, an anvil, a chest with two gods in it, and a reputation that has now been used in four different countries by lords who have never met him.

And in the eleventh month there came a letter out of the east. It was from a chieftess on the Volga called Nulzyava, who was holding a great tournament at Serdosk in the spring, and it was not a contract and there was no fee in it: it was an invitation, and the invitation said that a company with the name ours had two years of would be welcome in the lists.

He read it in the forge, standing up, with the tongs still in his hand, and he said: we are going east. And then he said the thing I have thought about more than the wolf: we will not be paid to win, and we will be paid a great deal more than we are paid to fight.

I am a soldier and I do not understand that sentence. I have written the whole of this chapter as a man who counts frontages and fees and mends what is broken, and on the last page of it the trade I have known for eighteen years is taken out of my hands by a chieftess on a river, and the company will spend a year riding east to be looked at.

Matako would have understood it. She is not writing this book any more, and she was standing at the forge when he said it, and she said afterwards, when I asked her what he meant: he means that a name is cheaper to make than an army and lasts longer. And then she said: write that down, it is as good as anything I wrote.

I have written it down. It is the end of this chapter and I am going to close the book here, and go and see whether my horse has been fed.

Notes

  1. Chrysogonos One of the five Greeks who came into the company at Constantinople in the ninth month of 879: Evanthia, Eupraxia, Hippostrate, Ioanna, and Chrysogonos. The record has him as a Roman Greek, a soldier since he was fifteen, and, at twenty-nine, a man with nothing to say about it. The birthplace inside the Empire, the nine companies and the two fleets are the book's; every number he reports about the company is checked against the roll. ↩
  2. present and on the roll A company's paper strength is a hundred and its present strength is whatever a clerk counts on the morning of a muster, and the two are never the same number. The record keeps them apart honestly: at Leibach in the first month of 882, one thousand and sixty men present against sixteen hundred on the roll; by the third month of 883, thirteen hundred and forty-five. The difference is desertion, sickness, death, and the ordinary human hope that nobody will count. ↩
  3. the two Petres There are two men called Petre in this war, and the book has been bitten before now by taking one man for another. Petre of the company, born 839, joined at Drăgășani in the fifth month of 883. Petre the enemy, born 863, twenty years old at Câmpulung in the second month of 884, is a different man with a different name-day and a different mother. The same caution applies to Alajos of the company, born 847, who joined after the battle, and Alajos the Hunter of Chapter VI. ↩
  4. Câmpulung The record for the twenty-second day of the second month of 884: fifteen hundred and ten against Iuga's fourteen hundred and thirty-eight, the enemy army destroyed, seven of ours lost, and the war settled by it — the largest one-sided result anywhere in the record, and the only battle in it after which the losing side never formed another army. Where the field lay, how the left was refused and where the horse came from are the book's. The arithmetic is the record's, to the man. ↩
  5. Kičevo and Debar Two towns in the western hills of the Macedonian country, on the old paved road that runs north of the Via Egnatia's line, which the company had already crossed twice as a pilgrimage on the way to Thessaloniki in 879 and 880. In the eighth month of 883 the record has no claim, no title and no war of his own — the first is four years away, in 887. What the chapter shows is a captain pricing ground, which is what such a man does a long time before he has a reason. ↩
  6. the wolf The record has one line for it: caught alone by a wolf on the twenty-eighth day of the tenth month of 884, and healed by the twelfth month. It does not say what passed between the two dates. The book has kept that gap open on purpose: no fight, no pelt, no omen asserted — only what the company decided afterwards, which is never the same thing as what happened. Laurentios, his trade, and the three days of teaching are the book's; the record has only that in the tenth month of 884 Laurentios taught Kasame and Edouinos what he knew. ↩
  7. the ford The record for the tenth month of 883 has one word for him and the word is wounded. It gives no place, no enemy and no cause, and the book has not supplied one: the three men who went with him say only that he went up the bank alone and came back, and the four stitches are in a chapter Matako wrote before this one, without comment. What happened at that ford is deliberately left where the record leaves it — open — and the company's own explanations of it, which are worse than silence, are reported as the talk they were. ↩
