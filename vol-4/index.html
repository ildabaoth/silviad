Volume IV of The Silviad

The Pilgrim

908 – 920

Kept by Matako, and then Laurentios. In this volume: Matako, and then then Laurentios.

Opened on the road south of Lonigo in the tenth month of 908 and closing, by the author's ruling, at the death of Edouinos Silviades. The smith keeps the book until the spring of 919, when the record gives her a miracle she cannot account for and then stops giving her anything at all; the soldier who walked to Rome with her writes the last chapter of the volume and the king's end. It is the volume of the four pilgrimages, of eleven wars of conquest, and of a company that loses more men than it takes.

Part I

Rome

908 – 909
1

Rome

908 – 909 · in the hand of Matako

Eight people and forty mules go south into the winter. At Orvieto two men join who will later hold the realm's only accolade and a county; at the Vaticano a Hellenic king is let into the yard of the Latin church and sold candles. In the spring of 909 he preaches the Wrath of Zeus in Rome and nothing happens; he begins, at fifty-eight, to learn Italian; he is courteous with the Pope for a quarter of an hour; Eupraxia stays behind for God and Kyriake leaves for nothing recorded, and Alajos the Temesite, who was an enemy at the muster in 880, dies in a pilgrim house. He comes home with a kingdom that ran without him, a prisoner, a rival, and a habit of the road.

Part II

The Duchies

909 – 910
2

The Duchies

909 – 910 · in the hand of Matako

A king comes home off a pilgrim road into a war within a fortnight. An Emishi horseman of sixty-four, thirty years in the company and unable to read, is made Count of Pitten and asks what he is supposed to do now. Lichtenfels costs three hundred and twelve men against one thousand two hundred and twenty-one, and a hundred and nine of the dead are Bavarians. Then the paper: three duchies handed to a young soldier and to two boys of four and three, three counties on the Main to a Greek ten months in the company, and a father leaving for Thessaly the day after, with a boy of eight who already understands.

Part III

Servia, and the Tournament

910 – 912
3

Servia, and the Tournament

910 – 912 · in the hand of Matako

A pilgrimage to Thessaly in which a king who was baptized for reasons of contract knows the responses by heart, and in which Pramda, the government of everything since 879, stays behind and gives the ledger to the smith. Bohemia is won without him; he spends a year in a bed coughing blood and the kingdom holds because it pays on time; Trubending destroys an army and takes a lord prisoner while he is being fed eggs. Then four deaths in five months, a daughter called Philostrate by a queen of thirty-nine, and the Recital at Győr.

Part IV

Tunis and Athens

912 – 914
4

Tunis and Athens

912 – 914 · in the hand of Matako

Two wars are declared and won while the King is not in the country: Slavonia, in a single day at Sisak and Zagreb, and Friuli, which takes Aquileia and keeps it. In between he crosses to Africa as a pilgrim in undyed wool, leads a prayer in a rite the record does not name, reads a stone nobody else can read, and brings home a court imam. Then a second pilgrimage, this one made for a purpose the record calls Reflection, to a city of two thousand people inside a ruin, where his heir of twelve asks where the schools have gone. Five hundred and nineteen days out of seven hundred and ninety-five, and a machine that has learned to run without him.

Part V

Moravia

914 – 916
5

Moravia

914 – 916 · in the hand of Matako

A war declared against a claimant the record met in 894 at sixteen and ends with him in irons at thirty-eight. Hohenau costs seventy-one men and is the largest battle in the book; Wien is one line with no numbers. At Kruszwica the King wins a second Recital at sixty-five, Balc dies in a tent at seventy-eight, and Benedetto — the soldier from the yard at Orvieto — is acclaimed First of the Vanguard with no land to his name. Then six Moravian counties to three children whose combined age is eleven, while the heir, at fourteen, is given nothing. And on the third day of the ninth month, Chrysogonos lets go of the strap.

Part VI

The Rhine

916 – 919
6

The Rhine

916 – 919 · in the hand of Matako

A second son is born and given his father's name while the first still lives, and the record never explains it. West Franconia is fought twice, Upper Lotharingia taken between them; Speyer and Koblenz bleed the company white, Seligenstadt costs three men, and Trier becomes a Roman city in the hands of a one-year-old. The heir is acknowledged twice in two days under a guardian nobody can identify, and becomes a champion at sixteen with a reputation for witchcraft; the King takes over his education himself. Then Momchil the Brute dies at court after fourteen years of being kept where the King could see him, and at Lautern a man of sixty-eight wins the most complete victory of his life, sits down on a wall, and says it was the last one he would do properly.

Part VII

The Pilgrim's End

919 – 920
7

The Pilgrim's End

919 – 920 · in the hand of Matako, then Laurentios

In March 919 the heir takes smallpox and by May he is dying; on the first day of the fifth month the fever goes in a night, and the woman holding his wrist cannot account for it and will not pretend to. On that same morning the kingdom declares its fifth war in five years. Then the ledger changes hands. A Greek named Laurentios writes the rest: Beichlingen, the only defeat in this history at Marburg, Lauterbach six weeks later; Mudra dead of the cancer she carried, Matako dead in her forge at sixty or sixty-nine and the record and the roll disagreeing; Shiina dead, and the record's three words for it. A holy war declared against a king of sixty-nine who had been ill since the first of May. Eleven hundred and eighty men dead at Gleichenstein, his last battle, watched from a chair. And on the twenty-eighth day of the sixth month, the end — with the peace a month away and no way to know whether he saw it.
