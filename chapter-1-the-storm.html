Chapter I

The Storm

866 – 867
In the hand of Edouinos Silviades

He was not.

The wind held until the middle of the night and then it did not, and by the time the light came there was no boat under us and no land we could put a name to. My father drowned before the light. The sea did not give him back, then or later; there is no grave anywhere with his name on it, and at this distance of years that is still the one thing we would change, if we were allowed to change one thing.

We put that first because a man who saves the worst for the end is arranging his facts to please himself.

The house at Naniwa1, in the eleventh month of the year 866, was two men and a storehouse, and the storehouse was worth more than either of them. There were warehouse hands in the daytime and none at night. There was a debt to a family in the eastern quarter, which was not large and had been running for eleven years, and a buyer in the north who had sent one letter and no money.

The voyage was his own and on paper it was sound. Iron in bars,3 bought in the west where it comes off the boats, sold in the north where there is never enough of it. A cargo that does not spoil, does not need feeding, and does not care how long it sits in a wet hold. He had made the same calculation a hundred times and been right perhaps ninety of them. The arithmetic was correct. It did not contain the season, and that is the whole trouble with arithmetic.

We were sixteen that winter and had been in the ledgers since we were nine. Silk by weight, dried fish by the barrel, salt by the measure, paper by the quire — and, between the lines, our own ability to be understood in three languages, which was the only stock the house owned that never needed a warehouse. We could weigh, we could read a manifest in two hands and a seal in a third, and we could argue about a price without raising our voice, which in that trade is the whole of a gentleman's education.

The boat was a coastal trader of the kind that beaches itself in a swell and is dragged off again by men standing in the water: flat-bottomed, one battened sail, a stern that lifts. She was hired for the voyage and hired cheaply, and the price is written in the ledger in his hand and underlined twice, which is the sort of thing a man does when he has decided something and does not want to be asked about it later.

For a month before we sailed he was not himself. We put it down to the market, because we were sixteen and because the market is always a sufficient explanation to a boy who has been told that the market is always a sufficient explanation. He wrote the manifest first and afterwards sat up with the front leaves of the ledger. We thought he was copying out accounts. The front leaves are not accounts. He knew, by then, that he was not coming back, or he knew something near enough to it that he wrote as a man writes who does not expect a reply.

✦

The northern sea road2 runs from the head of the Inland Sea up the western coast, port by port, and then through the strait where the island ends and the cold water begins. In summer it is a fat, dull voyage with a market at every third stop. In winter it is a different piece of water altogether, and the men who sail it in winter are not the same men who sail it in summer.

We went in winter for a reason that is written down and is still a bad reason. The strait closes early and opens late, and the first cargo through after the thaw sells for half again what the second sells for. The buyer at the far end wanted his iron before the roads came open, and had offered a price that punished anyone who waited. It was a good price. It was the price of a man who knew what he was asking.

The boat's people read the weather the way farmers read a field, and they were wrong twice before they were right. Our work was to keep the manifest dry and to count. We counted a great deal. Counting is what a merchant's son is for on a boat: it keeps him out of the way of the ropes and it makes him feel useful while the men who can actually sail it are doing so.

My father kept a log in the back of the ledger, in Greek, which the boat's people could not read, which was the point of writing it in Greek. Three of the entries are worth copying out:

“Ninth day. Contrary wind off Noto. Two days lost. The iron does not spoil, which is the only consolation cargo has ever given me.”

“Eleventh day. Fine. The master's people killed a porpoise and we ate it, and it was better than the food we had paid for. My son asked what we should do if the buyer were dead. Sell to the widow, of course. It is what a house does. He wrote that down, which I did not intend.”

It is a small thing, but we have kept it: he had never once, in eleven years, asked us for an opinion on a cargo and then written it down. We have decided, since, that he was teaching us at the last minute, the way a man remembers to light a lamp in a room he is about to leave.

✦

The storm did not arrive the way storms arrive in poems. There was no moment where the sky opened. The wind came round to the north-west during the afternoon, the sea went short and ugly rather than tall, and by dark the boat was working in a way that made the boards talk. The master put her stern to it and ran. That is the correct thing to do and it is why we are alive, and it is also why we ended up where we ended up, three days north of any place with a name in the manifest.

My father had seen the sea break the doors of the palace at Heian-kyō two winters before, and had spoken of it at every meal for a month. That was his idea of a storm, and it was the idea of a man who had watched one from a warehouse roof in a city.

The cargo killed us. Iron in a flat-bottomed boat does not drown you by weight, it drowns you by shifting: every roll the bars walked a little further to leeward, and every roll she sat deeper on that side. Two men went down into the hold with a bar between them and did not come up for a long time. We were not sent. We were sent below for the ledger, which was in the dry locker and was the only thing on that boat with no resale value at all.

That is the whole reason we are alive, and we have never been able to make it sound like anything but luck. My father went forward and we went aft, and aft is where the water left the boat.

My father's last entry is a sentence and a half:

“Thirteenth day. Shipped water twice. If the wind holds we can”

The ink is wet through to the next leaf. It is the second unfinished sentence my father left us, and we have never been sure which of the two he meant us to finish.

The mast went some time before dawn. After that there is not much that can be told in order: the sail in the water, the stern lifting, the master shouting names in a dialect we did not know we understood until we heard it. My father put the chest into our hands — the chest from the back room, which he had brought aboard without telling us, and which we have never known whether to call theft or inheritance. Then he pushed us at the rail, and said the thing a drowning man says, which is not a blessing or a charge but a piece of practical advice, and we have forgotten it.

We went into the water holding a chest that weighed more than we did. The sea, which had been trying to kill us since the afternoon, was extremely careful with that chest. We have thought about it a great deal since: what the sea respects is not weight but compactness, not men but things that were built to be carried.

We did not see him go. He was behind us and then the water was full of planks and rope and the sea was doing the thing it does, and there was nobody behind us. He could not swim. It is not a thing a trader in a river port ever needs to learn, and he had hired men to do the water for him all his life.

We came ashore in the dark, on a beach of black gravel, and lay in it like a fish until the cold made us move, and then found the stones and put the chest above the tideline and lay on it, because it was the only thing there that was not wet and not moving.

✦

At first light we counted, because counting was the only thing we knew how to do that did not require anybody else's consent. One chest, one ledger, a coil of rope, a barrel of salt fish that had split, and ourselves. The iron was gone. The silk was gone. The boat was a stern and half a side, lying in the shallows about a hundred paces out, and it would have been worth more than everything else if there had been any way to bring it in. The purse was in the chest and had been light when we left the river and was not heavier.

The sea kept the one thing we would have paid anything at all to have back, and returned the manifest, and we have never regarded that as anything but a joke in poor taste.

We did not weep on that shore. It would be easy to write that we did; it would make a better scene; it would not be true. What we did was count, and then walk the beach twice looking for cargo, because there was nobody else there to see us do it. Grief came three days later, in the middle of a sentence about rope, and it has come back at inconvenient times ever since, generally in the middle of a sentence about something else.

There was no village in sight and no smoke. There was, however, a path: gravel trodden into the sedge, and a place further along where somebody hauled boats out and left the rollers behind. We had the sense not to walk up it in the dark, and the sense not to be sitting on the chest when somebody came down it, and that second piece of sense is the only decision we have ever made that we would call wisdom.

They came on the second day, at the turn of the tide, two men and a boat better than the one we had lost. Fish-skin and fur, hair cut straight across the brow, a harpoon in the boat and a second one in the younger man's hand as though it had grown there. They had come for the iron. Everyone on that coast would have come for the iron.

The younger was Kekhan, twenty-two, and the older was Longun, forty, and they were of the shore people who fish and take seal and cut timber and answer to nobody in particular4. We had perhaps forty words of the shore trade tongue between us, badly learned at a wharf, and they had eleven, better learned. It was enough to establish that the iron was gone, that we were alone, and that we had no boat.

Kekhan did the talking. Longun stood in the boat with the harpoon across his knees and did not say a word for the better part of an hour, and we have since been told by people who knew him that this was his habit and not his opinion. The name Kekhan the Brave had been given to him by somebody who was not present at anything he did, and it reached us third-hand; we wrote it in the ledger because we could not pronounce what his mother called him, and we have used it for forty years, and he has never once corrected us.

The bargain took the whole of the afternoon, and it was a small one, which is why it held. They had a shelter on the ridge, a share in a winter camp, and no reason on earth to feed a foreign boy. We had a ledger, a hand, and the wreck of a boat lying in water we could reach at low tide. What we offered was the wreck: every bar of iron the sea gave back, ours to find and theirs to haul, in exchange for a place by a fire until the thaw.

Longun's question, when it came, was not about the iron. He pointed at the chest with his chin — the chest we had dug a hollow for and covered with sedge, which was not as clever as we had thought at the time — and asked what was in it. So we opened it, in front of two strangers, on a beach, because there was no version of that afternoon in which we did not open it.

A cup. A coin of a king nobody on that coast had heard of. A list of names in a hand older than the paper it is written on. One other thing which my father would not describe in writing and which we will not describe here. Longun looked at all of it for a while, then put the coin back in the chest and closed the lid with a care that surprised us, and said the thing that decided the next forty years of our life, which was that we had not lied about the cargo.

✦

That evening the three of us were one fire, which is not the same thing as one company. One of us had lost a father, a boat and a country in thirty hours. The other two had lost nothing, and had gained a debt, a foreigner, and a claim on a wreck. Somebody asked what we were to be called, since we evidently expected to be called something.

We wrote three words in Greek at the top of a clean leaf: hē endoxos synodia5. It means, approximately, the well-regarded company. We chose the word because it was the largest word we knew that promised nothing at all, and because the alternative was to write our own name at the head of a page with two strangers on it, and we were not ready for that yet.

In the language of the ledger it is the Distinguished Host. It was a lie of the kind that is not yet a lie, and we have been careful, in the years since, never to let it become one. When there were three of us it was absurd. It has been less absurd, at times, and we have never once been able to say it aloud in a hall without hearing how it sounded on a beach with the fire going out.

The year turned three days later6. We followed the register, and the register followed the court, and the court kept its own reckoning, and by that reckoning the first day of the first month of the year 867 fell on a morning of hard frost with the sea flat and grey and nothing whatever happening on the shore.

Once a month, on the same day, somebody in that house had lit a fire and said the names. That is the whole of the religion, as he wrote it: a chest, a fire and a list, no building, no priest, nobody to offend. It had been done behind a shut door for four generations in a country where the door being shut was the entire point.

That morning we carried the chest and the ledger up the shingle, out of the wind, and lit a fire of driftwood in the open, in daylight, in front of two men who thought we were doing something practical with the smoke. Then we said the names. We said them badly, too fast, in the wrong order, and one of them we could only get halfway, and we said them out loud, in the air, on a beach where anybody could hear.

Kekhan asked what we were saying. We tried to explain, and made a poor job of it, and he asked whether they were the names of dead men. We said yes, some of them, and some of them were the names of men who had been dead for nine hundred years and whose language nobody on that shore had ever heard spoken.

Longun listened to all of this with his hands in his sleeves. Then he said the only thing that could possibly have kept us on that coast, which was: say them again.

We said them again. He listened to the whole list a second time, and nodded, and went to see about the boat.

That is how a house with one surviving member, no walls and a debt at Naniwa began to be something other than a house. Not with a vow, or a battle, or a god. A man who had never heard of Macedon asked us to say the names a second time, and we did.

Notes

  1. Naniwa The port at the head of the Inland Sea, then the court's outward harbour and the country's chief market for goods from the mainland. The family's house and storehouse stood there for three generations. ↩
  2. The northern sea road The coastal route up the western side of Honshu and through the strait between the great island and the northern one. In the ninth century it was the only way north that did not mean crossing mountains, and it was sailed only in the months when the strait was open. ↩
  3. Iron Iron was worked in Japan, but not in quantities that could keep up with the demand of a growing provincial administration, and bar iron carried in from the mainland commanded a steady price. It was also the ideal cargo for a small boat: dense, unbribable, and impossible to spoil — though, as the chapter shows, not impossible to shift. ↩
  4. The shore people The chronicle never uses a name for them, and we have not supplied one, since the names by which they are known to scholars are later and were never theirs. They were fishermen, seal hunters and trappers of the coast and the island opposite, organized in small settlements with no overlord and no written law, which is precisely what made them useful and dangerous to the courts further south. ↩
  5. The Distinguished Host ἡ ἔνδοξος συνοδία — literally "the well-regarded company." The word carries no rank, no grant of arms and no legal meaning whatever. That is the point of it. ↩
  6. The first day of the year 867 The court and the register used a lunisolar calendar, so a date in the text corresponds only roughly to a date in ours. The correspondence given here is the translator's, and the reader should not put more weight on it than the manuscript does. The house kept the reckoning of the register because the register was where its name was written. ↩
