Appendix

On the sources

The chronicle is its own source and cannot be checked against anything. Where the wider world can be brought in, we have brought it, and it is worth setting down what is actually known about the places the book passes through.

The family's account of its own road east is unverifiable and internally consistent. Two anchors in it are historical: the extinction of Greek power in the Punjab in the early first century, and the merchant towns of the Tarim basin. Everything between them is tradition.

Japan in this period is Heian, governed by a court at Heian-kyō through provincial governorships, with its writ thin in the north. The court chronicles record a typhoon that broke the gates of the palace in 865, and — two years after our text begins, and a year after the book's author leaves Japan for good — the Jōgan earthquake and tsunami of July 869, which drowned some thousand people in Mutsu. He is not there. He was born on that coast, and he will hear of it late, from men who were.

The men who come down that beach are Emishi: a Japanese people of the north, in the court's phrase the people beyond the end of the road, who fought the court in the century before this book opens and lost, and were settled, or driven further north, in the decades that followed. The chronicle never uses that name — its author had not seen it written and had no reason to trust the court's word for anything — but the reader is entitled to know who these men are, so the notes say it.

The far north of the text is not empty. The Ainu place-name Chupka appears in Ainu epic. The two Nivkh in the company belong to the river-and-sea peoples of the Amur estuary and northern Sakhalin, with their own shamans and their own ceremonies; the book does not pretend to explain them, because the hands that kept it could not.

The text

The chronicle proper begins at Chapter I and runs, so far, to the morning of the second of January, 867.
