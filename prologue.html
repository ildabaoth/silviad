Prologue

The Father's Leaves

written at Naniwa, in the winter of 866
In the hand of Menandros Silviades

I am Menandros, of the house of Silviades, and I keep the ledger of this house at Naniwa. If you are reading this, you are my son, and I have not come back. There is nobody left in this country who can tell you what I am about to tell you, which is the reason for all of it.

I am putting it at the front of the ledger, where the accounts begin, because I am a coward about conversation and a competent man with a pen. A boy who has just lost his father will not sit still for a speech. He will read anything that is left lying about in front of him, and he will read it more than once.

Edouinos: the register says we are Hayashi. Hayashiya1 — a grove, a stand of trees. My great-great-grandfather's great-great-grandfather was called Silvius, which is Latin for the same thing, and the family has been translating itself ever since. In the year we came into this country we translated it one last time, into Chinese and then into Japanese, and paid a clerk to write it in a book. That is the whole of our conversion. It was an act of paperwork.

Before that we were Greeks in Bactria2, and before that Greeks in Macedon, and before that — a soldier of Alexander's army, or his son, or a man who followed the pay. The story changes depending on who in the family is telling it, which is how you know it is true.

For three hundred years there were Greek kings in India. They kept theatre and coinage and the old gods and, in the end, not much else. The last of them lost his kingdom in the year ten3, and after that there was no Greek country anywhere east of Syria. I am named for the greatest of those kings. Menander4. He ruled a country I have never seen, and argued with monks, and is remembered by men who have never heard of me.

Our people did not flee. I want you to understand that, because it is the only part of this that is a habit rather than a story. Nobody was driven out. They simply kept going the way they were already going — the Tarim towns5, Dunhuang, the long road into China — a generation at a time, trading, and then serving, and then trading again. Four hundred years of it. Somewhere in those centuries the family stopped writing Greek and began writing Chinese, and nobody thought to record the day.

We came to this country in my grandfather's time, because the sea road is good and the north is thin of competition and the clerks here will register anyone who pays. We married here. And once — once — we did not, and that was your mother, and she was the last person in this house who was not pretending.

There is a chest in the back room. It is not treasure; do not open it hoping for treasure. In it are a cup, a coin of a king nobody in this country has heard of, a list of names in a hand older than the paper it is written on, and one other thing which I will not describe in writing. Once a month, on the same day, someone in this house has lit a fire and said the names. That is the whole of our religion in practice: a chest, a fire, and a list. There is no building. There is no priest. There is nobody to offend and nobody to complain to.

Now the part I have been walking around for eleven years.

There is no one else. Not in this country. Not in China. Not in any of the towns I have traded in for forty years, and I have been in a great many of them. I have never met another Hellene6 who was not standing in this house. Your mother was the last, and she is dead, and the chest is now the only congregation there is.

You are the last one I know of. I am the second to last, and I am fifty-one, and I am crossing to the north in the eleventh month7.

So: put it back. I do not know what that means. I am a trader and my imagination runs to cargo, and when I try to think of a nation I think of a warehouse with better walls. But I know it does not mean keeping a chest in a back room until the last man who knows what is in it dies and the room is let to strangers.

If you do nothing else with your life, make it so that there is one place in the world where these names can be said out loud, in daylight, by more than one person, without anybody having to close a door first.

Your mother's name was Eudokia. Nobody in this house says it. I have let that happen, and I am sorry for it, and I am telling you now because a name that is not said is a name that is being lost on purpose. You were named for her. That is why your name is not a Japanese name, whatever the register says.

I am sailing on the eleventh with a hold of iron and a buyer at the far end who has not paid a deposit, which is the sort of arrangement I have made a hundred times and regretted perhaps a dozen. If I am back before the thaw we will not speak of any of this. If I am not,

The text breaks off here, mid-sentence.

Notes

  1. Hayashiya (林屋) A grove; a stand of trees. The Latin silva means the same, and the Greek hyle means the same again, which is how a family can change country three times and keep its name without ever keeping its language. The register's version is the third translation and the only one anyone wrote down. ↩
  2. Bactria The country between the Hindu Kush and the river Oxus, today northern Afghanistan, conquered by Greek armies after Alexander and held by Greek kings for two centuries. It was the easternmost Greek state there has ever been, and it was already a memory when this family's road began. ↩
  3. “the year ten” The writer reckons from the Seleucid era, as his family did, and does not name the era, because everyone he expected to read it knew. It corresponds to the last years before the Christian reckoning, when the last Greek kingdom in India was extinguished. He gives a round number and he is approximately right. ↩
  4. Menander Menander I, called Soter, the most successful of the Greek kings of India; his coins circulated for centuries after his death, and one of them is in the chest described below. He is also the King Milinda of the Buddhist dialogue, which the writer of this leaf certainly never read. ↩
  5. The Tarim towns The chain of oasis cities north and south of the Taklamakan desert, on the roads between China and the west: Kucha, Khotan, Turfan and their neighbours. They were the marketplaces of the silk road, and they were where a Greek family in flight from the south would have had work. ↩
  6. Hellene The word the family uses for itself, and the word the translator has kept, rather than "Greek" or "pagan," neither of which means what the manuscript means. It is a word about descent and habit, not about belief; the religion described a few lines later is a chest, a fire and a list of names. ↩
  7. The eleventh month In the reckoning of the court, which used a lunisolar calendar, the eleventh month fell in midwinter. The writer gives no day. The next leaf of the book does. ↩
