Chapter I

The Storm

866 – 867
In the hand of Edouinos SilviadesThe boy alone: one survivor, one chest, one beach. The company does not exist yet.

He was not.

The wind held until the middle of the night and then it did not, and by the time the light came there was no boat under me and no land I could put a name to. My father drowned before the light. The sea did not give him back, then or later; there is no grave anywhere with his name on it, and at this distance of years that is still the one thing I would change, if I were allowed to change one thing.

I put that first because a man who saves the worst for the end is arranging his facts to please himself.

The house at Naniwa1, in the eleventh month of the year 866, was two men and a storehouse, and the storehouse was worth more than either of them. There were warehouse hands in the daytime and none at night. There was a debt to a family in the eastern quarter, which was not large and had been running for eleven years, and a buyer in the north who had sent one letter and no money.

The voyage was his own and on paper it was sound. Iron in bars,3 bought in the west where it comes off the boats, sold in the north where there is never enough of it. A cargo that does not spoil, does not need feeding, and does not care how long it sits in a wet hold. He had made the same calculation a hundred times and been right perhaps ninety of them. The arithmetic was correct. It did not contain the season, and that is the whole trouble with arithmetic.

I was sixteen that winter and had been in the ledgers since I was nine. Silk by weight, dried fish by the barrel, salt by the measure, paper by the quire — and, between the lines, my own ability to be understood in three languages, which was the only stock the house owned that never needed a warehouse. I could weigh, I could read a manifest in two hands and a seal in a third, and I could argue about a price without raising my voice, which in that trade is the whole of a gentleman's education.

The boat was a coastal trader of the kind that beaches itself in a swell and is dragged off again by men standing in the water: flat-bottomed, one battened sail, a stern that lifts. She was hired for the voyage and hired cheaply, and the price is written in the ledger in his hand and underlined twice, which is the sort of thing a man does when he has decided something and does not want to be asked about it later.

For a month before we sailed he was not himself. I put it down to the market, because I was sixteen and because the market is always a sufficient explanation to a boy who has been told that the market is always a sufficient explanation. He wrote the manifest first and afterwards sat up with the front leaves of the ledger. I thought he was copying out accounts. The front leaves are not accounts. He knew, by then, that he was not coming back, or he knew something near enough to it that he wrote as a man writes who does not expect a reply.

✦

The northern sea road2 runs from the head of the Inland Sea up the western coast, port by port, and then through the strait where the island ends and the cold water begins. In summer it is a fat, dull voyage with a market at every third stop. In winter it is a different piece of water altogether, and the men who sail it in winter are not the same men who sail it in summer.

We went in winter for a reason that is written down and is still a bad reason. The strait closes early and opens late, and the first cargo through after the thaw sells for half again what the second sells for. The buyer at the far end wanted his iron before the roads came open, and had offered a price that punished anyone who waited. It was a good price. It was the price of a man who knew what he was asking.

The boat's people read the weather the way farmers read a field, and they were wrong twice before they were right. My work was to keep the manifest dry and to count. I counted a great deal. Counting is what a merchant's son is for on a boat: it keeps him out of the way of the ropes and it makes him feel useful while the men who can actually sail it are doing so.

My father kept a log in the back of the ledger, in Greek, which the boat's people could not read, which was the point of writing it in Greek. Three of the entries are worth copying out:

“Ninth day. Contrary wind off Noto. Two days lost. The iron does not spoil, which is the only consolation cargo has ever given me.”

“Eleventh day. Fine. The master's people killed a porpoise and we ate it, and it was better than the food we had paid for. My son asked me what we should do if the buyer were dead. Sell to the widow, of course. It is what a house does. He wrote that down, which I did not intend.”

It is a small thing, but I have kept it: he had never once, in seven years, asked me for an opinion on a cargo and then written it down. I decided, since, that he was teaching me at the last minute, the way a man remembers to light a lamp in a room he is about to leave.

✦

The storm did not arrive the way storms arrive in poems. There was no moment where the sky opened. The wind came round to the north-west during the afternoon, the sea went short and ugly rather than tall, and by dark the boat was working in a way that made the boards talk. The master put her stern to it and ran. That is the correct thing to do and it is why I am alive, and it is also why I ended up where I ended up, three days north of any place with a name in the manifest.

My father had seen the sea break the doors of the palace at Heian-kyō two winters before, and had spoken of it at every meal for a month. That was his idea of a storm, and it was the idea of a man who had watched one from a warehouse roof in a city.

The cargo killed us. Iron in a flat-bottomed boat does not drown you by weight, it drowns you by shifting: every roll the bars walked a little further to leeward, and every roll she sat deeper on that side. Two men went down into the hold with a bar between them and did not come up for a long time. I was not sent. I was sent below for the ledger, which was in the dry locker and was the only thing on that boat with no resale value at all.

That is the whole reason I am alive, and I have never been able to make it sound like anything but luck. My father went forward and I went aft, and aft is where the water left the boat.

My father's last entry is a sentence and a half:

“Thirteenth day. Shipped water twice. If the wind holds we can”

The ink is wet through to the next leaf. It is the second unfinished sentence my father left me, and I have never been sure which of the two he meant me to finish.

The mast went some time before dawn. After that there is not much that can be told in order: the sail in the water, the stern lifting, the master shouting names in a dialect I did not know I understood until I heard it. My father put the chest into my hands — the chest from the back room, which he had brought aboard without telling me, and which I have never known whether to call theft or inheritance. Then he pushed me at the rail, and said the thing a drowning man says, which is not a blessing or a charge but a piece of practical advice, and I have forgotten it.

I went into the water holding a chest that weighed more than I did. The sea, which had been trying to kill me since the afternoon, was extremely careful with that chest. I have thought about it a great deal since: what the sea respects is not weight but compactness, not men but things that were built to be carried.

I did not see him go. He was behind me and then the water was full of planks and rope and the sea was doing the thing it does, and there was nobody behind me. He could not swim. It is not a thing a trader in a river port ever needs to learn, and he had hired men to do the water for him all his life.

I came ashore in the dark, on a beach of black gravel, and lay in it like a fish until the cold made me move, and then found the stones and put the chest above the tideline and lay on it, because it was the only thing there that was not wet and not moving.

✦

At first light I counted, because counting was the only thing I knew how to do that did not require anybody else's consent. One chest, one ledger, a coil of rope, a barrel of salt fish that had split, and myself. The iron was gone. The silk was gone. The boat was a stern and half a side, lying in the shallows about a hundred paces out, and it would have been worth more than everything else if there had been any way to bring it in. The purse was in the chest and had been light when I left the river and was not heavier.

The sea kept the one thing I would have paid anything at all to have back, and returned the manifest, and I have never regarded that as anything but a joke in poor taste.

I did not weep on that shore. It would be easy to write that I did; it would make a better scene; it would not be true. What I did was count, and then walk the beach twice looking for cargo, because there was nobody there to see me do it. Grief came three days later, in the middle of a sentence about rope, and it has come back at inconvenient times ever since, generally in the middle of a sentence about something else.

There was no village in sight and no smoke. There was, however, a path: gravel trodden into the sedge, and a place further along where somebody hauled boats out and left the rollers behind. I had the sense not to walk up it in the dark, and the sense to move the chest off the shingle into the sedge and sit a little way off from it, and that second piece of sense is the only decision of my life I would call wisdom.

They came on the second day, at the turn of the tide: four men and a boat better than the one I had lost. Fur and fish-skin, hair cut straight across the brow, harpoons in the boat, iron at their belts. They had come for the iron. Everyone on that coast would have come for the iron.

They were Emishi4: the shore people, who fish and take seal and cut timber and answer to nobody in particular. I had perhaps forty words of the shore trade tongue, badly learned at a wharf, and they had eleven, better learned. It was enough to establish that the iron was gone, that there was one of me, and that I had no boat.

The bargain took the whole of the afternoon and it was a small one, which is why it held. They had no reason on earth to feed a foreign boy. I had two hands, a ledger, and the wreck of a boat lying in water that could be reached at low tide. What I offered was the wreck: every bar of iron the sea gave back, mine to find and theirs to haul, in exchange for food and a place behind their fire for as long as the work took.

One of them asked what was in the chest. So I opened it, in front of four strangers, on a beach, because there was no version of that afternoon in which I did not open it. A cup. A coin of a king nobody on that coast had heard of. A list of names in a hand older than the paper it was written on. One other thing which my father would not describe in writing and which I will not describe here.

They looked at all of it for a while, and then the oldest of them put the coin back and closed the lid with a care that surprised me. Nobody asked for anything. That was the first time in my life that anyone had looked into that chest and not wanted something out of it.

✦

The iron was the whole of that winter. They cut the hull apart in the shallows over eleven weeks, at low tide, in weather that would have killed me if I had been doing it alone; I kept the account of every bar that came out, because the division was going to be argued about and a written account ends an argument. Two hundred and six bars. I have the number still.

What I learned there was not seamanship. It was that these people took a boat apart the way a household takes apart a wardrobe: everything is a thing that can be used again, nothing is rubbish, and the man who says a piece is worthless is the man who has to carry it. I had been taught to count what a thing cost. They counted what a thing would do.

I wintered in a shelter of my own making, on the ridge above the beach, and I was not one of them and never pretended to be. They tolerated me because I could write and could reckon and because I was useful about the iron. Usefulness is not belonging. I learned that at sixteen and it has been the foundation of every house I have built since.

✦

The year turned5 on a morning of hard frost, with the sea flat and grey and nothing whatever happening on the shore. I followed the register, because the register was where my name was written, and by its reckoning that morning was the first day of the first month of the year 867.

Once a month, on the same day, somebody in that house had lit a fire and said the names. That is the whole of the religion, as he wrote it: a chest, a fire and a list, no building, no priest, nobody to offend. It had been done behind a shut door for four generations in a country where the door being shut was the entire point.

That morning I carried the chest up the shingle, out of the wind, and lit a fire of driftwood in the open, in daylight. Then I said the names. I said them badly, too fast, in the wrong order, and one of them I could only get halfway, and I said them out loud, in the air, on a beach where anybody could have heard.

Nobody heard. There was nobody on that shore who could have understood a word of it, and the people who had been cutting my father's boat into pieces were three ridges away, asleep. I had done the thing he asked for and it had made no difference at all: one man, saying names to a fire, on an empty coast, in a language that had come nine hundred years to die in his mouth.

So I sat there until the fire went out and understood the rest of the charge. He had not asked me to say the names. He had asked for a place where they could be said by more than one person. That is not a thing a man can do alone on a beach in the north. It is a thing that requires people — and not the people who happen to be nearest, but people who will stay.

Which was when I stopped thinking like a trader's son with a drowned cargo, and started thinking like the last man of a house with a list of names and nowhere to say them.

I went south in the third month, with the chest, the ledger, two hundred and six bars of iron that were no longer mine, and a vocabulary of forty words. I did not know the way. I could not have told you what a county was, or a border, or where the court's writ stopped being real. I knew that south was where the people were, and that west was where the names had come from.

That is the end of what I can tell you as a boy on a beach. Everything after it is the story of a company, and it needs a different voice than mine.

Notes

  1. Naniwa The port at the head of the Inland Sea, then the court's outward harbour and the country's chief market for goods from the mainland. The family's house and storehouse stood there for three generations. ↩
  2. The northern sea road The coastal route up the western side of Honshu and through the strait between the great island and the northern one. In the ninth century it was the only way north that did not mean crossing mountains, and it was sailed only in the months when the strait was open. ↩
  3. Iron Iron was worked in Japan, but not in quantities that could keep up with the demand of a growing provincial administration, and bar iron carried in from the mainland commanded a steady price. It was also the ideal cargo for a small boat: dense, unbribable, and impossible to spoil — though, as the chapter shows, not impossible to shift. ↩
  4. The Emishi The name is the court's, not theirs: it means, more or less, “the people at the end of the road,” and the court used it for everyone north of its writ who had not yet been counted. They were fishermen, hunters and horse-breeders of the northern coasts, living in settlements that acknowledged no governor and paid no register — which is precisely what made them useful and dangerous to the courts further south, and which in the century before this book opens had cost them a long and losing war. The chronicle does not use the word; its author wrote down what he could pronounce. ↩
  5. The first day of the year 867 The court and the register used a lunisolar calendar, so a date in the text corresponds only roughly to a date in ours. The correspondence given here is the translator's, and the reader should not put more weight on it than the manuscript does. The house kept the reckoning of the register because the register was where its name was written. ↩
