Chapter V

The Irons

879 – 880
In the hand of Tokifusa, and then MatakoThe ledger's keeper wrote as far as Athens and stopped at a fire in the fourth month of 880. The company's smith finished the chapter, because she was the only person in it who had made something the chapter is about.

The camp under the wall was the largest the company had ever made. Four hundred and some men, eleven carts, a forge with a roof, a wheelwright's bench, ninety mules, and the twelve Zhanmadao at the gate of it, and behind all that the largest wall that any of us had ever stood under and the city it was built to keep out. We had come four thousand miles to camp in a field, and the field was full.

I had sixty-three men in the Zhanmadao now and called them the twelve. The twelve were the men the Marquis gave us in 871 and most of them were still alive; the rest had been recruited out of Armenia and the Persian roads and one place and another, and they had been given to understand on the day they joined what the twelve were and what they were not. This is how a regiment is made. Nobody writes it down. It is a thing a sergeant says once and a hundred men remember.

Matako kept the forge and the wheelwright kept the bench, and the fine work by that autumn was anything that needed two hands and an eye. The Ayrudzi kept to themselves and to their horses, which is what noble cavalry does, and their captain was a man with no name worth writing for two years and then a name I learned in Thrace and will come to.

I have to put down a death before I put down the city, because the city is the part everybody asks about and the death is the part that changed us.

Sabbas was a muleteer out of Trebizond, a Greek with three languages and a bad back, and he had been with us three years. He bought the barley. That was the whole of his office: at every way-station between the frontier and the sea, Sabbas bought the barley and the hay and the beans and he fixed the price with the man selling it, and the men ate and the mules ate and nobody counted the sacks, because counting the sacks is what a suspicious man does and we had come a long way being the other kind.

He had been taking a tenth. Not the tenth of a thief who means to be rich — a tenth of a man who has decided he is owed something and has worked out the smallest number that will pay it. Over three years it came to a sum you could put a roof on. He had written the numbers in a little book of his own, which I found afterwards, and the columns were correct.

Dehanagadevi kept the stores. She was thirty-five years old that autumn and she was out of the Indian years and she had been with the company since the Ganges country. She counted what came in and what went out and she punished herself on a schedule and other people without one, and if you had asked any man in that camp who was the hardest person in it he would not have said the young master and he would not have said the twelve.

She found the tenth in the fifth week of the road out of Anatolia and said nothing. On the second night of the ninth month, in the camp under the wall, she took a knife out of the forge and went to the mules and killed him, and she did it quietly, and she dragged him out beyond the pickets so that the thing would be found outside the camp and not in it. Then she counted what he had on him into the chest, wrote the sum against his name, and sat down by the fire and waited for me to come round with the watch.

I have known men to lie about such a thing for the whole of a morning. She did not lie about any part of it, including the part I would have left out. She gave me the little book. She said the account was now correct. She said it in the flat way she said everything, and then she sat there with her hands folded and waited to be told what happened next, and I understood that she genuinely did not know, and that she had not thought about it, and that this was the worst thing about her.

The company woke up to a corpse and a correct account and the hardest woman in it sitting by the fire. Half of them thought she had done right. I want that in the book plainly, because it would be easy to write a camp full of honest men who were shocked, and there was not a camp full of honest men, there was a camp full of people who had been on the road for twelve years and had opinions about thieves.

Edouinos came out at first light and looked at the man and then at her, and he did not shout, which surprised the men and did not surprise me. He asked her three questions and she answered all three in a sentence each. Then he asked her the one question she had not thought about, which was who had given her the right, and she said she had taken it, and he said that was what he had thought.

He imprisoned her that morning. There is no gaol in a caravan. There is a cart with a cage on it for dogs and there is whatever the smith can make. He sent for Matako and told her what he wanted, and Matako went to the forge she had built with her own hands at Cedi and made two irons and a chain and a collar, and brought them to him, and did not say one word about it to anybody, and I have never asked her what it was like and neither has anybody else.

She was not chained to a tree. He had the irons put on her at the assembly, in front of the whole company, the four hundred and the twelve and the Ayrudzi and the muleteers, and he had the sentence read out by me because I am the one who reads things out. It was four sentences long and the last of them was that her name came off the roll until the company should decide otherwise. Then he told her that she would walk to the sea with us in her irons and eat at the fire and keep her hands busy, because a prisoner who is left behind is a prisoner who is killed by somebody else and he was not going to have two deaths entered in one week.

That is the whole of it. It is not the scene anybody expects. There is no speech in it about law, and there is no mercy in it that a man could admire from a distance.

I have thought about the order of his sentences for years now. He did not imprison her for killing Sabbas. Any captain of that company would have had to do something about the killing and every man in it knew that the something would be light, because Sabbas had robbed them and they had eaten what he stole. He imprisoned her for the sentence she had passed. She had decided, alone, in the night, with a knife from the forge, that the company had no law but her arithmetic, and he put her in irons for that, and he did it in front of everyone, and the only man in the camp who understood it at the time was a Japanese who kept the lists.

I opened a column in my book that week. There is one column in a thousand pages with one name in it, and the name at the head of it is Dehanagadevi, and under the name there is a date and a sum and the word *owing*, and it stayed open for three years.

*** ## The City

We went in on the sixth day. The gate he had read the board at on his first morning was one of a dozen, and the street inside it was not a street, it was a room with a roof of its own and men selling bread in it before the sun was over the wall. I had been in Chang'an. I want that understood before I describe anything, because Chang'an is the largest thing I ever expected to see and I had the same argument with myself in both cities, which was that no city is a city; it is a hundred villages that have agreed on a wall.

Constantinople is built on water on three sides and on the fourth side it is built on the wall, and the wall is the reason for the city and the city is the reason for the wall, and you cannot see the arrangement as a whole from anywhere inside it. I asked a porter where the old temples were. He did not understand the question. I asked a priest, in the Greek I had learned off the young man, and the priest understood it and did not like it, and he told me that the temples were the churches and the churches were the temples and that a man who wanted to see what the Greeks had been should go and look at the foundations under the floor.

That is the thing the young man had come four thousand miles for and it took a doorkeeper to say it to him. The old city is under the new one, and the new one has been using the old one as a quarry for five hundred years. There is a bronze horse in the square that came off a temple, and a column with a snake on it that came off a tripod, and there are statues in the walls and statues in the foundations and statues in the lime kilns, which is where most of them went. A man in the Forum of the Ox sold me a finger of marble the length of my hand and told me it was Praxiteles and charged me a price for it that was either a joke or a bargain and I have never decided which.

Edouinos walked that city for a month and did not write in the ledger. He had his Macedonian coat out of the chest on the second day and he wore it in the street like a man wearing a flag, and the people looked at him the way a city looks at a foreigner who is dressed as their own grandfather.

The word is the thing. In that city a Hellene is a man who has not been baptised. It is an insult in the mouth of a bishop and a piece of legal shorthand in the mouth of a clerk and it means a man outside, a man in the dark, a man whose gods were a scandal and are now a joke and a name in a list of old errors. He had carried the word four thousand miles in his own mouth as the name of what he was. He heard it here out of other mouths for a month before he understood that it had never been his to carry, and then he understood it all at once, at a table, over a bowl of something with lentils in it, and he put the spoon down and went out.

I was with him. He stood in the street in the rain with his hands at his sides and said, in Greek, that they had kept the language and lost the word. Then he said the four words of the house, which are not for the street, and a woman with a basket looked at him the way you look at a man talking to a wall, and we went back in and finished the lentils.

That was the sixteenth day of the ninth month. Everything else in this chapter is what a man does after that.

There was a tournament in the eleventh month, out beyond the Gate of the Spring, on the flat ground where the armies parade. It was given by a Roman magnate for a wedding, and the men who rode in it were Franks and Italians and two Varangians who were not really riding anything, and the whole thing was foreign to the city and the city came out to watch it anyway.

I do not know how to write a tournament. It is a foreign word for a thing we had no word for, and the closest I can get is that a hundred men agreed in advance to pretend to try to kill each other for the entertainment of people who had paid to watch, and then to be paid themselves, and that the pretending was extremely serious. There was a fee to enter. He paid it. There was a fee to have your arms carried and your horse held. He paid that too. By the time he rode into the lists he had spent on one afternoon more than the company had paid for its last three contracts' worth of barley, and he was so pleased with himself that I could not be angry about it.

He rode in the Macedonian coat over mail, and the horse was the grey he had bought at Adrianople, and Shiina had spent a month teaching him to sit a horse in armour, which is a different skill from sitting a horse and one he did not have. He was drawn against a Frank in the first pass. The Frank put him on the ground. He got up, and the ground was muddy, and he was covered in it to the shoulder, and the crowd laughed at him in Greek, and he laughed too, and then he went and did it again.

Four passes. He was unhorsed in three of them and in the fourth the Frank's lance broke and the judges gave it to him, and he took the prize, which was a ring worth less than his entry fee, and he wore it for two years.

Shiina laughed at him for a month. That is not a figure of speech: every evening in the camp for a month, in front of whoever was there, Shiina would describe the second pass to somebody who had not heard it yet, and the description got better every time, and by the end of it there were details in it that had not happened, including a goose. Edouinos let him. He would stand there and take it and add one correction, always the wrong one, and the men would laugh at both of them.

A man who can be laughed at in front of his own company is a man the company can love, and I had not known until that month that he could be. Twelve years he had been careful with them. Careful is not the same as loved. He had had their respect since the frontier and he got the other thing in a wet field outside the Gate of the Spring, by falling off a horse three times and getting up.

Two of the Varangians came to the fire the same week, and then a man who sold horses and could read, and by the end of the eleventh month there were five Greeks in the company.

I will set them down as they came, because they are the last people in this book who joined us for no reason, and after them everybody who joins has a reason and the reasons matter.

Evanthia was twenty-five and out of a family that had run out of money two generations before she was born, and she was beautiful in the way that costs a woman something in a city, and she had a temper she was not able to hide and a knife she was able to hide. Eupraxia was twenty-eight, and stubborn, and had been a steward's clerk, and had left a household in Nicaea in a manner she would not describe. Hippostrate was twenty-two and had read everything and could read the old letters on the stones, which is how she paid for her supper for the first month. Ioanna was thirty-five and had commanded men — not a company, a watch, forty men at a gate in a Theme — and had been dismissed for a reason that she gave once and never again. Chrysogonos was twenty-nine and had been a soldier since he was fifteen and had nothing to say about it.

None of them believed one word of what he believed. That is the whole joke of those five and it took me a year to see that it was not a joke. They were the first people in twelve years who could say the names of the house in the language the names were in, and they said them correctly and without any feeling at all, the way you say the name of a street you live on.

He hired them for the pilgrimage because he needed men who spoke to the men at the gates. He kept them for eleven years because they were good at their work. And somewhere in the second year he stopped being able to tell which of them would still be with him if the money stopped, and by then it was too late to care about the answer.

On the twentieth day of the tenth month we struck the camp under the wall and went west to Didymoteichon, which is a day and a half of good road, and which we took four days over because the carts were loaded for a pilgrimage and not for a war.

*** ## The road to Athens

A pilgrimage is a contract with no client. That is what I wrote at the head of the page and I stand by it. The company was paid to go to Athens by nobody, for nothing, in the wrong season, with a prisoner in irons and four hundred men to feed, and the men were glad to go, and the reason they were glad is the reason no ledger has a column for.

Didymoteichon is a market town in the low country with a hill over it and a garrison in the hill, and it was full of pilgrims, because that is what a road to a holy place does to a country: it fills the inns. We were the largest party on the road by a factor of ten and the smallest by reputation, which is a thing that happens when your captain is a Hellene and your soldiers are Chinese and your interpreter is a Georgian who lies for pleasure.

The road from there to Thessaloniki is the old Roman road, the one that goes from the city on the strait all the way west to the sea on the other side, and it is two thousand paces of cut stone with a ditch on both sides and milestones with numbers on them. We walked on a road that had been built by men who are all dead and were maintained by an empire that no longer exists, and it was better than any road we had walked on since China. I put that in the ledger because it is the kind of thing that belongs in a ledger: a road is a contract between a government and everybody who will ever come after it, and this one was still being honoured six hundred years after the government that signed it had stopped existing.

We went up through the passes in the last week of the tenth month and it rained for eleven days. The Hebros was in flood. Two mules went into it and one of them came out. We ate the other one, or most of it, and the men who had been on the Ganges said it was better than the fish.

Thessaloniki is the second city of the Roman world and it has a wall and a harbour and a great many churches and a fair in the autumn, and we spent the fair and left in the first week of the eleventh month with more money than we came with, because a company of four hundred men sells to a fair as surely as it buys from one.

After that the road goes west along the coast and then south, and there is a stretch of it between the mountains and the sea that is the worst road in this book. It is not the longest and it is not the highest and nobody dies on it. It is the one where the carts have to be taken to pieces and carried and the mules go one at a time and a mile takes a morning. It took us nine days to do forty miles and I wrote nothing in the book for nine days because there was nothing to write except the number of miles, and the number of miles was the same every day.

He walked the whole of that stretch. He had a horse and he walked. On the fourth day I asked him why, and he said that a man who rides into Athens has been carried there, and he wanted to be able to say he had walked, and I wrote that down and then I crossed it out, because it is the kind of sentence a man says to be admired later and I do not keep that kind of sentence. I have put it here instead, where it can be seen for what it is.

*** ## Athens

Athens is a small town with a large past and a rock in the middle of it. We came in sight of the rock on the first day of the first month of 880, in the afternoon, from the north, and the whole company stopped in the road and looked at it, and the men who had been told what it was told the men who had not, and half of what they said was wrong.

The rock has a church on the top of it now, in the biggest of the old temples, and the town at the foot of it is a town of six or seven thousand people who speak Greek and grow olives and do not think about any of it. There is a market in the ruins of a library. There are houses built into the seats of a theatre. There is a temple of Zeus in the south-east of the town that was never finished, eighteen columns of it standing in a field of weeds, and a hermit living in the corner of it who had been there for nine years and did not want company.

He went up the rock on the second morning and came down in the evening and did not say what he had found. He went up again on the third. On the fourth day he called the company together in the field by the unfinished temple and led the prayer, which he had never done before, and which he did in Greek, and which the five Greeks stood through with their heads down and their eyes open.

I have heard men say since that it was a fine thing. It was not a fine thing. It was a hundred and eighty men standing in a weed field in the rain saying four words they did not understand, led by a man in a good coat, with a prisoner at the back in irons who was saying them too and who had a better right to them than most of the congregation. What it was, was the first time in the history of that company that the man who owned it did something that did not make money.

The statue was bought on the fourteenth day. It is not a great statue. It is a Zeus of the small kind, bronze, a foot and a half high, seated, with the eagle worn down to a lump, and it had been dug out of a field four miles away and had come to a dealer in the town who sold it to pilgrims and to Franks and to anybody else. The dealer wanted a price for it that would have kept his family for two years. Edouinos bargained with him for an afternoon, in Greek, and got him down by half, and paid, and carried it back to the camp himself without letting anybody else touch it.

I was at the bargaining. It was the best afternoon's work I ever saw him do. He had the man's whole position worked out in a quarter of an hour — that the statue could not be sold to a Christian, that the Franks would not come until the spring, that the dealer had a loan and a brother-in-law — and he took him apart politely, one piece at a time, and then he paid him a price that was still fair and let him keep his dignity, and the dealer went away happy with a bad deal, which is the highest art of the trade and the only kind of magic I have ever seen him work.

He put the Zeus in the chest with the cup and the coin. That is three cups and a coin now, or a cup and a coin and a statue, and a pressed geranium between two pages of the ledger. He was twenty-nine years old that winter and the chest was fuller than it had ever been and there was still one thing in it that he had never shown me and never would.

The winter was wet and the company was idle and idle companies break. He put them to work. There was a stretch of the old road south of the town that had gone in the floods and he contracted with the town to mend it, four hundred men for six weeks for a sum that was a joke, and the men were paid and the town was grateful and the men stopped fighting each other. I entered the contract in the book. It is the only entry in that whole year with a client's name in it.

Then he got ill. It began as the fever he had had in India and it was not the same fever. He was down for three weeks in the second month, in a room in the town, with Matako and the five and a monk, and the men outside the door keeping the door, and there was a week in the middle of it when he did not know anybody.

I sat with him two nights of that week. He talked. I am not going to write down what he said because I did not understand it and because a man in a fever has no responsibility for his sentences. But I will write down one thing, which is that on the second night he said a name I had never heard him say and answered a question nobody in the room had asked, and then he sat up and said, in Greek, that it was the wrong statue.

Matako was there. She was the only one in the room who did not look frightened, and when I asked her afterwards what she made of it she said the fever, and she said it a half-beat too late, and I have been in this trade eleven years and I know what a half-beat is.

Biàn came to him on the thirteenth day of the second month, three days after the fever broke, and asked to be let go.

He was forty-two years old and he was the company's joiner, which means he was the man who made the wheels true and the axles straight and the boxes that the money travelled in, and he had been with us since before I came. He had come out of the far south of China with a boat and a bad marriage and had never once in eleven years said what he thought about anything. He asked to stay in Attica. He had found a house of monks on the mountain behind the town and he wanted to be one.

Edouinos asked him the only question worth asking, which was whether he believed it. Biàn said he had believed it for two years and had been waiting for a place to be able to say so. Then he said the thing that made the young man sit down, which was that he had not come to the company to be a joiner and had stayed because of the fire and the names and the walking, and that a man could not walk forever and be nobody's.

He let him go. He gave him the price of a cell and a year's bread out of the chest, which is the first money that ever left that chest and did not come back in another form, and he gave him the company's leave in front of the whole camp, and then he watched him walk up the hill with a bag and did not say anything to anybody for the rest of the day.

I want to be exact about the size of it. Four hundred men and eleven years and the first one who ever asked to stop was allowed to stop. Every man in that field understood what had happened, and every man in that field knew it had cost the company a joiner and the captain a friend, and there was not one of them who would have taken it away from him.

Biàn was not at the fire the next night and he was not at the fire any night after that. There is a gap in the list where his name was, and I have left it, because a list with a name taken out is a list with a hole in it and the hole is the record.

The pilgrimage was completed on the first day of the fourth month. I looked the word up. It means the thing was finished, and it does not mean anything else, and there is no column in the ledger for what it was.

The three months in that town cost the company four hundred and some pounds of silver in food and wages and rent and the mending of a road, and brought in one contract and a statue and a great deal of credit with the town and a monk on a hill and one man gone. If I put those numbers in front of a merchant he would take the book out of my hands and look at my head. I have put them in front of myself many times since. The numbers are the numbers. He was not wrong.

On the fifteenth day of that month he and Matako sat at the far fire until the small hours with the book between them and did not talk much, and when they got up she was crying, and he was not, and neither of them ever told me why.

I asked her once, years later. She said it was the fever and the statue and a thing she would not write down. That is her business and not mine, and it is not my hand that has to write what comes next.

In the hand of Matako

*** ## The road north

I am Matako. I am thirty years old, I have been the company's smith since Kushiro, and I learned the rest of what I know at Tsunenao's elbow in the years when he still had elbows worth learning from. Before this month I had never written anything longer than a list of what a cart needs to go another hundred miles. He asked me to write in Thessaloniki in the fifth month, in the yard behind the inn where the carts were standing with their wheels off, and he asked me the way he asks for a thing he has already decided about, and I said yes, because there was nobody else in that company who would say the true part and he knew it.

Tokifusa has the date of that fire right and the rest of it wrong. We were not talking about Athens. We were talking about what a man is allowed to keep.

I made the irons. That is the thing I have to put down before anything else, because it is the only part of this chapter that nobody else in the company can write. He sent for me at first light and told me what he wanted, and I asked him one question, which was whether they were to hurt. He said no. That answer is an hour of work at a forge and any fool can make a chain that hurts; the collar has to be turned inside and filed and the hinge has to sit where the wrist does not find it in the night, and a woman who has been chained does not thank you for a chain that is beautiful, she thanks you for one she can forget. I did not ask him who they were for. Everybody in that camp knew by midday, and nobody knew it before I had finished.

I have thought about the weight of them more than I have thought about Athens. Two irons and a collar and eleven feet of chain come to nine pounds, and Dehanagadevi walked from the wall to Athens and back in nine pounds, which is two and a half months of walking with a stone in each hand, and she never once asked to have them taken off and she never once fell behind. Whatever else she is, she is the hardest person that company ever had in it, and I have known some iron in my time and I know what it takes to carry a thing without complaining about it.

He told me a thing in Athens that he had told nobody. I am not going to write it down, and I did not have to be told it, because I had known since Cedi and had been waiting two years to find out whether he knew it himself. He is the kind of man who keeps a thing so well that his own soldiers think he is only lucky. I am the other kind, and we found that out about each other in a ruined town in the rain, and that is what the fire was, whatever Tokifusa told you.

Three things I taught him in Attica and one thing he taught me, and none of the four is going in this book. What I will say is this. A man who prays in a field in the rain in front of a hundred and eighty men is not hiding anything by praying. He is hiding something by praying in Greek, which is a language in which a man can say two things at once and be understood to have said the one he meant.

The pilgrimage ended in the fifth month in Thessaloniki and we came back up the road with the carts lighter and the men quieter. Four hundred men went into Attica to see a temple and came home having watched their owner give away a joiner and a year's bread and walk nine days up a bad road rather than be carried along it, and they were a better company for it, which is not a thing that can be entered in a column and is the only reason I am able to write this chapter at all.

I cannot write the way he does. I have read this book twice, all of it, in the cart, and the two men who wrote it before me are better with a sentence than I will ever be, and both of them have written things about me that are kind and not quite true. I will write what I made and what it cost and what a thing weighs. When I have a number I will give the number. If that makes a worse book, then it makes a worse book, and the company will still have a true one.

There is one column in that ledger with one name in it, and I made the irons that put her there, and I have never said one word about it to anybody in eleven years. I am saying it here because it is true, and because I am the one who has to write what comes next.

In the eighth month of that year the men stopped calling him the young master. But that is the next chapter, and this is the end of this one.

Notes

  1. the Ayrudzi Armenian noble cavalry: armoured riders out of the country north of Lake Van, hired in 877–878 and kept because they were worth what they cost. In a company of traders they were the only men who had been soldiers before they were hired, and they never let anybody forget it. ↩
  2. the lists The word the Franks and Italians brought with them for the ground a tournament is fought on, and by 879 the Roman east had learned the thing itself from the Latins who came through it: an afternoon of hired men in armour, a fee at the gate, and a prize worth less than the fee. ↩
  3. the Via Egnatia The Roman road from the strait at Constantinople west across Thrace and Macedonia to the Adriatic. It was maintained, in the sense that nobody had destroyed it, six centuries after the government that built it had stopped existing — which was, to a caravan master, the single most impressive thing in the Roman world. ↩
  4. the lime kilns Where most of the statues went. Marble burns to lime, and lime is what a town needs to build a church or a wall or a floor, and there was no reason in the tenth century to keep a pagan figure when there was a reason to build. A man who wanted to see what the Greeks had been was shown the foundations under the floor, and told to be glad of them. ↩
  5. the Olympieion The temple of Zeus in Athens, begun under a king six hundred years before and never finished: columns in a field, a corner with a hermit in it, and no roof it ever had. It was the largest temple in Greece for the whole of the time it stood, and it never held a service. ↩
