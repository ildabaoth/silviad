Chapter VII

The Statue of Hades

881 – 883
In the hand of MatakoThe company’s smith, continuing from the last chapter, because she went on the road, sat in the churches, made the irons and kept the count of what the year cost.

I ended the last chapter at Calafat in the eleventh month of 881, with the ledgers open and the carts half loaded, and I said the fee had gone into a road. This one I have written in four places: at Calafat before we left, in Rome in the spring, at Calafat again in the autumn of 882, and here at the City this winter with the fires out and the men asleep and nothing to do but count. Where the counting is interrupted by a thing I saw, I have written the thing.

The fee was four months old when he spent it. That is the fact I keep coming back to. Tarasiy paid us in the seventh month for a war we had won in two afternoons, and by the eleventh month there was money in the chest and money owed and a road ordered, and the road ate all of it before we saw the Alps. A wiser captain keeps a reserve. He did not keep a reserve. He bought a pilgrimage the way other men buy a horse they cannot shoe.

He gave a reason and the reason was the one the priests would have wanted. He said he was going to Rome to be made better at believing. I have known him twelve years and I will tell you what I think: he was going to Rome because Athens had not been enough, and because a man who has bought one god wants to know whether there is a market in them.

We left on the eleventh. I could name every one who joined us on that road and I have written their names down, and I cannot tell you what any of them thought they were joining, because a company does not ask that question of a man until the second winter.

The carts went with us. That is a thing nobody who has not kept a forge understands: you do not leave the anvil. I have carried that anvil from Kushiro across two rivers and a desert and half the world, and where the company goes the anvil goes on a cart with the wheels off twice a year and the charcoal in sacks under the shaft, and the day I leave it behind is the day I am not a smith any more.

We went up the Morava in weather that could not decide. That is the country where the road is the river and the river is the road, and in the last month of a year the two of them argue. There were eleven days of it and on the ninth a cart went into the water and we got it out with the mules and lost a sack of nails and one of the saws, and I sat on the bank afterward with my feet in the mud and thought about how far Kushiro is.

Ana became his friend on the twenty-first, at a fire, in the way these things happen, which is to say nobody announced it. She had been riding with us nine days by then and was not yet of us, and she had the particular quiet of a person waiting to be told she can stay. She had come up out of the hills with a sword and had been in one war already and had come out of it with no opinions, and the Captain asked her three questions and she answered two of them and he said stay, and she stayed.

She came into the company properly at Debrc in the first days of the twelfth month, and I put her on the roll under the anvil because that is where I put everyone I intend to keep an eye on. She was not much of a hand at anything yet and did not pretend to be, and by the end of that road she could set a rivet and would not do it wrong to save time, which is more than I can say for men who had been at it longer.

Debrc is a flat place with a cold church in it and a river on two sides. We were there eight days, and it rained on six of them, and the Captain went to the church twice. He did not go in the way a man goes in who wants to be seen going in. He went at the hour when there is nobody there, and stood at the back, and came out, and said nothing about it, and I know because I was mending a hinge on the door of the shed behind it and could see the door of the church, and I have never told him I saw.

That is the whole of his religion as far as I have watched it, and I have watched it twelve years. He is not a man who talks to gods. He is a man who goes into their houses when they are empty and stands in them. The father's book is full of the other kind of talking and I think he knows he cannot do the other kind, and so he does this instead, and it is either the most honest faith in the company or the least, and I have not decided which and I do not expect to.

Leibach and the regiments

We came down into Leibach on the first day of 882, and Leibach is where the road stops being a Balkan road and starts being a Roman one that has been repaired by somebody who cared. There is a river with three bridges on it and a hill with a tower and a market that sells iron in bars instead of by the piece, and I bought iron there and did not have to argue about the price with a man who had never seen a bar in his life.

On the same day the companies grew. That is what the paymaster wrote and it is true and it is also nonsense, because a company does not grow on a day, it grows in the eight months before the day, and what happens on the day is that the clerks stop lying to themselves. Every kind of soldier we had went from three companies of a hundred to four. The armoured foot, both companies of them, the Armenian horse, the light horse, the swordsmen. Nobody was recruited in Leibach. What happened in Leibach is that the men we had been paying as three hundred were counted as four hundred because for two years there had been men in the column who belonged to nobody's company and everybody's work.

It is the Captain's own doing and he would not say so. He had gone from a man who was hired to a man who was asked for, and asking had a price, and the price was that he had to be able to say how many he had. He said it at Leibach and it was one thousand and sixty men, in companies counted now for sixteen hundred, and it was true, and the moment it was true it was also a promise.

Koinos joined us there. He is thirty-nine and he is a Greek, and he came to us with a bundle of charts in oiled cloth that he would not let anybody else carry, and he can read them. He was the first man in the company who could tell me what the sea was doing, and I did not know I wanted to know until he told me.

And a girl joined us at Leibach, and I am going to put this down plainly because everybody else in the company has decided not to mention it. Andjela of the house of Belojevic was eight years old in the first month of 882. She came into the company with an old servant and a chest and no explanation, and the Captain put her on the cart with the wives and the boy and told the paymaster to put her down at half a share, and the paymaster did, and nobody in the company ever asked him why, and I have heard four men give four different reasons and all four of them were making it up.

What I know is what I saw. She was given the cart with the cooking things because it is the warmest and the least jolted, and by the time we were in the hills she had learned eleven of our words and one song, and in Rome she sat in a cold church for an hour by herself with her hands in her lap and nobody could get her to come out. There is a scene in that and it is not mine to write. I put her down at half a share and I have never once regretted it and I have never once been able to explain it.

Yordanka became his friend on the sixteenth, which is the feast of nothing that I know of, at a ford, in the rain, over a mule that had gone lame. She is nineteen and she had been with the carts three weeks and had said perhaps forty words to anybody, and the mule was hers to look after and she could not get it up, and he got it up and then stood in the rain and let her tell him how she had been told to do it and how she had done it and what she thought had gone wrong, and he listened to the whole of it.

That is the thing about him that no chronicle will ever put in. He listens to the whole of it. A man who has twelve hundred men and a war behind him and a road to Rome in front of him stood in the rain and heard a girl of nineteen explain a mule. He does that with everybody, and it costs him an hour a day, and it is the reason the company is what it is and the reason he has never once been able to be wrong in front of them.

The hills before Rome

The road from Leibach runs west and then it stops being a road and becomes a series of decisions. In the last week of the first month we crossed the hills into Italy in weather that took two mules and a wheel and a boy's two fingers. There is no way to make a mountain crossing interesting in a ledger, so I will give you the ledger: nine days, one cart abandoned, eleven men with the flux, four days of no fire because there was nothing dry in the world, and one night in a shed where the snow came in through the roof in a line as narrow as a finger and lay on the floor in a line, and we all slept either side of it and by morning the line was still there.

Camaldoli is a high place in the Tuscan hills with a spring in it and a hospice kept by men who take no payment and ask no questions, and we came to it at the beginning of the second month with the carts and the mules and the anvil and thirty-one of us. They gave us bread and a fire and a roof and a place to put the sick, and they asked nothing, and I have thought since that the whole of Christian charity as it is practised is that: a fire, and no questions, and no bill.

We stayed eleven days there and it was the best eleven days of that road. I set two wheels and did a job of work on a plough for the men who kept the hospice, and they had never seen a woman with a hammer and got over it in an afternoon, and the boy went out with their boys and came back with snow in his hood and a word for a thing I did not have a word for.

In that time the Captain went out alone three times and came back with his boots wet, and the third time I asked him what he went to look at and he said the road, and I said the road is that way, and he said not that road. And then he did the thing he does, which is to say the true thing an hour later and in a different voice, and what he said was: I have been on a road for sixteen years and I do not know what a man is when he stops.

I did not have an answer. I have been on the same road for fourteen of the fifteen. A smith does not ask that question because a smith's answer is a cart and a hammer and the next thing that is broken, and there is no end to that, and I think it is why he keeps me near him.

Rome

We came into Rome on the nineteenth of the second month of 882, in the afternoon, in the rain that Rome has in February and which is not like other rain because it is warm and it comes off the stone and does not go into the ground. And I want to put down what I saw, because everybody has seen Rome and nobody has seen it arriving with an anvil on a cart.

It is not a city. It is a city that has been taken apart and a town built in the pieces. You come along the road and there are things on either side of you the size of a company's camp, walls with the arches still in them, a place where a whole hill of building has been quarried down to the floor, columns standing in a field with grass up to the capitals where nobody has put a church or a wall or a shed, because there are so many of them that they are not worth using. A man from Kushiro would have wept or gone to work. I did the second thing in my head for about an hour and then I stopped, because there is no end to it and Rome will beat you.

The Vatican is a hill outside the old walls with its own wall around it, a mile of it, and inside that wall is the church and the square and a town of the small business that feeds pilgrims, and we put the carts in a field below the wall because a company of fourteen hundred does not go inside anything. The rest of us walked up. He walked up with the rest of us, in the coat, with the mud of the road on him and no one to announce him, and we stood in the square in the rain and looked at a church the size of a hill.

On the twentieth he bought the badge. That is the whole of it: a piece of cast lead the size of a thumbnail with a face on it and a pin, sold out of a basket at the door by a man who had four hundred of them and would not take less than a penny, and the Captain bought four and gave one to me and one to Ana and kept two. I still have mine. It is on a nail above my table and it is worth nothing and I would not sell it for a mule.

On the twenty-first he led the prayer in the morning, in the field below the wall, in Greek. That is the fact. What it looked like was this: the company standing about in the wet in the half light in the way men stand when they have been told to be somewhere and have not been told why, and him in the middle of it saying the names, and the men answering where the answer goes. Not all of them. The Christians did not answer, and stood, and I watched their faces and there was nothing in them but cold. Kurumpruhoda answered. Tokifusa answered. Nine or ten of the Greeks answered, and Dehanagadevi did not, and Ana did not, and I did.

There is a thing I have to write here and I have put it off for a page. In Athens, two years before, he had done the same thing and I was not there for it, because I was at the carts with the saws. I have read what was written about Athens and it says a holy place where the rite was not his alone. This was not that. This was a Greek in a wet field outside the wall of the Latin church, saying the names to a company that was half foreigners, and there were four hundred people within a hundred paces of him going about their morning, and not one of them turned round.

That is what Rome is. It has seen everything and it has four hundred years of being told that the old gods are finished and it no longer bothers to argue. I found that harder than a mob. A mob would have been a fact about the world. The indifference was a fact about us.

On the twenty-eighth of that month Andjela sat in the church for an hour. I have written that already and I am not going to write it again, except for this: when she came out she was neither crying nor pleased, and she went and got on the cart and asked for bread, and whatever it was she did in there she did alone, and the Captain did not go in after her, and I think that was the wisest thing he did in Rome.

I had my own hour on the fourteenth of the third month, in the afternoon, when the light comes in the high windows sideways and the dust stands in it. I am not going to make a mystery of it. I sat down because my back hurt and I stayed because nobody asked me to do anything, and I thought about Kushiro and the town I learned my trade in and the man who taught me and the fact that I have been thirteen years in a company of men I can name and there are three of them I would call for in the night, and that is a good number, and I sat there until my back stopped hurting, and then I got up and went and put a new edge on a knife for the paymaster.

The wrath of Zeus

On the twenty-first of the third month, in the morning, in the vegetable market by the round church that used to be the house of all the gods and is now the house of all the martyrs and still has a dead man's name cut into the front of it in letters a foot high, he preached the wrath of Zeus to about forty people.

I was there. I want that understood, because I have read a version of this since, written by a man who was not there, and it had him in the Forum with a crowd and a magistrate and an argument. There was no magistrate. There was a market with cabbage in it and a man selling fish out of a barrel and forty people, and eleven of the forty were ours, and the rest were Greeks who lived in that quarter and had come to see what the noise was.

He did not shout. That is the first thing. A man who preaches in a market generally shouts, and shouting is the sound of a man who wants a crowd, and he did not want a crowd, and he got one anyway, which is the way of it. He stood with his back to the round wall and spoke in Greek in the voice he uses when he is telling the company something it will not like.

What he said, as near as I can give it, was this. He said that the wrath of a god is not weather and not misfortune and not the falling of walls. He said it is the name men give to a debt when the creditor has stopped asking. He said the gods are owed three things and the first is the oath and the second is the guest and the third is the fire, and that a city which keeps none of the three may be rich and walled and safe for four hundred years and will still be owed, and that Rome had kept two of the three better than any city that has ever stood and had broken the third so long ago that it called the breaking a custom.

Then he said the thing I have thought about since. He said: every man in this market believes that the gods of this hill were finished by the gods of that hill, and he said that is what the Christians say and it is what the philosophers say and it is what the emperors said, and there is one thing none of the four has ever said, which is that the gods do not die of being disbelieved. They die of being unpaid. And then he said: I am not here to tell you they are alive. I am here to tell you what you owe.

That is a merchant's sermon. I want to be fair to him and I will be: it is the sermon of a man who has spent sixteen years on contracts, and it has the weakness of every contract, which is that it assumes the other party is there to be owed. But it had one thing in it that a priest could not have said, and the Greeks in that market heard it, and two of them came back the next morning to hear it again and he was not there, and I have wondered ever since what they did with the rest of their lives.

Nobody arrested him. A boy of about twelve threw a fish head and missed. A man in the crowd asked him whether he was a Jew and he said no, and the man asked what he was then, and he said a Hellene, and the man laughed in a way that was not unkind and said there is no such thing, and he said I know, and the market went back to being a market.

When he came back to the carts he was not pleased. I had expected him to be pleased, because I have watched him win battles and he is pleased after battles in a way I do not like. He sat down on the tail of the cart and put his hands on his knees and stayed there a while and I brought him water and he did not drink it. And I said: what. And he said: nobody stopped me. And I said: did you want them to. And he said: I wanted it to cost something. And then he said a thing I am going to write down because it belongs in a book of mine and not in one of his: a thing that costs nothing is a thing nobody believes, and I have been saying the names in fields for two years now and the only thing it has ever cost me is the company's opinion of me, and the company's opinion of me is worth less than a fish head.

He did not preach again. That is the end of it and nobody should make more of it than that. But I have put it here in the middle of the chapter because it is the middle of the thing he was doing in Rome, and because a man who goes to Rome to be made better at believing and comes away having preached once in a market is a man who has learned something, and I do not think it was the thing he went for.

What the gods cost

On the sixteenth, five days before the market, he sold goods. That is the phrase on the roll and it is accurate: he sold exotic wares, which in this company means the last of the Chinese silk that had come with us out of the east and never been sold because it was too good to sell, and two of the little iron boxes I make for keeping needles dry, and a bale of Ottoman wool that had been under a cart since Galați. He sold them in one afternoon in the pilgrim town for forty-one gold, and the wool cost us twelve, and he came back and did the sums on the edge of the paymaster's table and was pleased in the ordinary way.

He needed the money and he was not going to say so. The road to Rome had eaten the fee and the chest was thin and we had fourteen hundred men in a field below a wall in February, and a company that cannot pay in March is a company that is not a company in April. There is no drama in that. There is a ledger, and I have the ledger, and if anybody wants to know what a pilgrimage costs, the answer is forty-one gold in silk and a winter of salt meat.

On the twenty-eighth he took me on an excursion to see the sights, and I want to be honest about the excursion, which was: he wanted to go and look at a thing and he wanted somebody with him who would not talk, and I am the person in the company who will not talk.

We went out along the old road to the place where the aqueducts come into the city, and there are seven of them arriving in the same field, on arches, one above another, some of them working and carrying water and some of them broken off in the air in the middle of a span, and men had built sheds against them and were living in them. And he stood and looked at that for a long time and then he said: they built it and then they stopped being able to build it, and they did not stop being able to build it because they were conquered. And I said: then why. And he said: because nobody was paying for it. And I said: so it is the same sermon. And he said: it is the same sermon.

On the fifteenth of the fourth month he bought the statue of Hades.

That is the fact and I have written it as flat as I can and it will not stay flat, because a man who has bought Zeus in Athens does not buy Hades in Rome by accident. The Zeus he bought in Athens was the father, the sky, the thing that is over you. This one is the other brother, the one who holds what is under the ground and does not answer, and the dealer had it in a cellar near the river with three other things and a bucket in the corner, and it is carved wood, and it is a little over two feet, and it has the helmet that they put on him so that you cannot see his face, and one hand is broken off at the wrist.

The dealer wanted ninety and got fifty-eight and the Captain paid it without a murmur, which for him is a surrender. I asked him on the road back why that one, out of the four things in that cellar. And he said: because it is the only one that is not asking for anything.

I have thought about that answer for a year. The father's book is a book of a man who was owed and was not paid and never stopped asking. And the son went into the cellar of a Roman dealer and bought the one god in the city who does not ask, and carried him home on a cart, wrapped in the wool bale, for eleven hundred miles.

The two of them are in the chest now. Zeus and Hades. He does not take them out. In four years he has taken the Zeus out twice, both times at night, and I know because I sleep near the chest and I am a light sleeper and the lid of that chest has a particular noise. The chest is the only thing he owns that is not a coat or a sword or a mule, and it holds two gods and the father's leaves, and where the company goes it goes, and when the company is in the field it is under the anvil cart, and that is as much theology as this chapter is going to have.

The news from Severin

The news came in the fourth month, and it came the way news comes to a company that has been paid and has left: late, second-hand, from a man who had it from a man.

Domoszló the Brute brought twenty-seven hundred men against Tarasiy's two hundred and twenty-two, below the same walls we had held for two afternoons in the summer before, and there was no battle in it worth the name. The war ended on the first day of the fifth month. Alajos the Hunter holds Severin. The Temesite holds the county he came for, which is what a war is for, and it took him fourteen months and two destroyed armies to do it, and he did it anyway.

I have looked at those numbers for a long time. Two hundred and twenty-two. When we left in the eleventh month, Tarasiy had the fort and a year of pay in the chest and the river road making him money every week. I do not know where the rest went. Some of it went home to the hills, some of it went over, and some of it went into the ground, and I have no way of knowing which, and neither does anybody.

The company that won both battles lost the war. I am going to write that out because it is the thing this chapter is actually about and I do not want it lost in the statues. We beat Alajos twice on that field and killed eleven hundred and seventy-two of his men and lost nine of ours, and we went away paid and pleased, and because we went away, the man we had beaten went round behind us and took the county in a year. That is not a betrayal and nobody did anything wrong. That is what happens when a company takes a fee and then takes a road: the fee buys an afternoon and the road costs a year.

He read the letter three times. I was there for two of them. He did not say anything to me and he did not call the officers and he did not put it in the book, and the next morning he went up to the church on the hill and was in it a long time, and when he came out he said one thing to nobody in particular, which was: I was on a pilgrimage. And then he said: write it down as that.

Half of the men thought he was grieving for Tarasiy. He was not; there had been one letter and one table and that was the whole of it. Half of the rest thought he was ashamed. I do not think it was shame either, or not the useful kind. It was the discovery that a thing he had done in the seventh month of 881 had finished happening in the fourth month of 882 without him, and that he had spent a winter proving to himself that he was a Hellene, and a county had changed hands while he did it.

The road back

The pilgrimage finished on the twentieth of the fifth month. He visited the city properly on the twenty-ninth, whatever that means, and Atenulf di Brescia joined us in Rome in the sixth month, and Valdamerca joined at Avezzano in the seventh, and by then we were not a pilgrimage in any sense that a priest would recognise.

Atenulf is out of Brescia and he is twenty-nine and he came to us because he had been in three lords' service and had run out of lords. He is the first man we ever had who had been properly trained to command more than a company, and it took him four months to stop speaking to the Captain as though the Captain were a lord, and when he stopped, the company got smaller and better in the same week.

Valdamerca came to us at Avezzano out of the Abruzzi hills with a horse and a bow and a bad temper and has stayed. She was thirty-one and she had been widowed twice and she could not be lied to about anything, which is a hard quality in a company and a valuable one in a winter.

The mountains take you back the way they took you in, and this time it was summer and there was grass and the crossing took six days instead of nine and nobody lost a finger. In the eighth month we were in Prozor, in the hills above the Bosnian country, and that is where the paymaster wrote him down as an experienced pilgrim, and it is the only line in sixteen years of rolls that has ever made me laugh out loud.

And in the ninth month we came to Severin.

We were not going there. It was on the road and the road was on the river and the river was the way home, and so we came down the bank in the last of the afternoon in the ninth month of 882 with fourteen hundred men and a cart with an anvil on it, and we went through the town in column because that is what a company does, and there was a garrison on the wall that was not our friend's garrison.

They stood on the wall and watched us and we went through and nothing happened. Nobody spat. Alajos the Hunter was not in the town. His men had been paid to hold a wall and we had been paid to hold it once, a year before, for a lord who is now a man with two hundred and twenty-two men, if he still has them, and probably he does not.

I walked my cart through the gate I had sat outside of with a saw and a pair of tongs in the seventh month of 881 while eleven men were brought in on hurdles. There is a place twenty paces inside that gate where the ground is lower than the road. I have no idea why. I noticed it then and I noticed it now and I have never in my life been able to explain to anyone what I noticed.

The Captain did not stop. He did not look at the wall and he did not send anybody up to the fort and he did not say anything at all, and when we were a mile past the last house he turned round in the saddle and looked back at the town once, and then rode on, and Tokifusa, who was next to me, said under his breath: do not ask him.

I did not ask him. I am asking him in a different way, five hundred pages of it, and he knows it, because he asked me to write this and he gave me the ledgers and he has not once asked to see a page.

Then east: Lovech in the ninth month and Nikopolis in the tenth, two short journeys out and back to settle what could be settled about a contract that had died while we were in Italy, and Calafat again in the eleventh month, and the same ford and the same sandbanks and the same ferrymen with the same complaints.

It is a strange thing to come back to a place where you buried three men and find a man mending a boat and a woman selling onions and nothing whatever to say to either of them. I have done that in eleven places now. The third time is the last time it does anything to you.

The fireside

We were at the City by the first of the twelfth month, in the fields to the west of it where the company had camped in the ninth month of 879 and where the ground is still bare in a circle the size of a camp because fourteen hundred men have slept on it twice.

The revelry began on the second. I have to explain what that is, because the word in the roll makes it sound like an orgy and it was not. It is the thing a company does in the dead weeks of a winter when there is no war and no road and money in the chest: the fires are kept up all night instead of let down, and the wine is issued instead of sold, and the men are not told to be anywhere in the morning. It lasts as long as the money and the weather hold and then it stops. The goal written down for it was a fireside chat, which is the paymaster's way of writing down that a hundred men will sit round a fire and talk for six weeks.

Tokifusa gave the host a gift on the eleventh. He is forty-eight years old and he has kept the roll for as long as there has been a company to keep it for, and what he gave the Captain was the roll itself: fair-copied, in his own hand, every name that had ever been on it, the living in one column and the dead in another at the back with the month they died and the place, bound in two boards with a cord. He had been making it for two years. He said, in front of everybody: now you have the count. And the Captain took it and could not say anything, and stood there holding it, and the company waited, and eventually he said: read me the back one. And Tokifusa read the back one. All of them, from the top. It took the better part of an hour and nobody left.

That is the company. I have written about the battles because the battles are what the book is asked for, and if anybody wants to know what the company actually was, it was an hour of a man of forty-eight reading the dead out of a book to a hundred men round a fire, and no one leaving.

On the eighteenth Kasame and Álmos taught each other their customs. He is a Hungarian and she is not, and their customs are, as far as I could tell from the other side of the fire, chiefly a matter of which way you turn the cup and who drinks first. It went on for two hours and ended with four men arguing about it and one of them wrong.

On the twenty-fifth Tokifusa read to Boril out of a book, at night, by the fire, in the way a man reads to somebody who cannot read. Boril is forty-three and has been a soldier since he was a boy and cannot read a word of any language, and Tokifusa has been reading to him since the sixth month, and it was the fifth night of it that I happened to watch. There is nothing else to say about it and I have thought about it more than any other thing in this chapter.

Evanthia and Aghsartan were by the same fire for three nights running in the first month. She is twenty-eight and quick and out of a family that ran out of money two generations before she was born; he is sixty-one and a Georgian and a zealot and has never in his life been quick. I do not know what they talked about. I do know that on the third night she was crying and he was not doing anything about it, and that is sometimes the right thing, and he is a man who has outlived most of the fires he ever sat at and knows when to do nothing.

On the fifteenth my lord Tokifusa admired Shiina's oratory, which is a sentence I have written down exactly as the paymaster gave it to me and which means that the two of them stood by a fire and Shiina talked and Tokifusa enjoyed it. Shiina is thirty-eight and vain and the best-looking man in this company and has been talking since he was twenty-two, and Tokifusa is the only man in the company who can listen to Shiina for an hour and enjoy it, because Tokifusa has no vanity at all and therefore nothing to defend.

On the twenty-ninth of the first month Evanthia drank under the night sky with Dehanagadevi.

I am going to write about that carefully. Dehanagadevi has been in this company since the Ganges country and she has been in irons since the ninth month of 879, and I made the irons — wrists, throat, and a chain to the ankle — and I made them well, because I do not make things badly. On the twenty-ninth the chain was off. I noticed because I notice chains; it is a professional habit and it is also a habit of mine. She sat on a rock outside the light with Evanthia and they drank whatever it was for an hour and a half and Evanthia did most of the talking and Dehanagadevi did all of the listening, and then she went back and the chain went back on.

Who ordered that, I do not know. I know it was not me and I know it was not the officer of the watch, because I asked him and he said the Captain had spoken to him that afternoon. I did not ask the Captain. He has three people in this company he tells things to and I am one of them and he has not told me this, which means either it is not a thing or it is a thing he is not ready to have written down, and in either case it is not my page.

The rest of it was small. Tokifusa explained the architecture of the aqueducts to Nawang for the better part of a night in the second month, and Yáoyuè read to the same Nawang a week later out of a book in a language he did not know, and Rivai cooked a dish from his own country at the end of the second month and put it in front of a hundred men who did not want it, and about forty of them ate it and about twelve of them liked it, and he was satisfied, which is more than most men get.

The revelry ended in the third month. The money ran out in the way money does: not a decision, an arithmetic. The wine stopped being issued and the fires began to be let down at midnight and the men started mending their own boots, and one morning the Captain came out and looked at the camp and said the word that ends every winter, which is: we go.

The count

So here is the count, and it is the last count of this volume, and I have taken it three times and got the same answer twice.

When the company went up the road from Chupka in the winter of 866 there was a boy and a chest and a handful of men and a trade name bought from a widow. Seventeen years later, in the third month of 883, in a field outside Constantinople, the company has thirty-three companions and thirteen hundred and forty-five men present of sixteen hundred on the roll, and twenty companies of soldiers of five kinds, and eleven carts, and one anvil, and a chest with two gods in it and the leaves of a dead man's book, and it has a name that a lord in Wallachia will use in a contract and a name that a paymaster in Rome has heard.

He is thirty-three years old. He holds no land. He has never held land. Everything in that count is a thing that can be carried and can therefore be lost, and he knows it, because he is the man who wrote out the inventory in the first place and has been adding to it since he was sixteen.

The road west is finished. I do not mean that it is over; I mean that the west has been reached. He went to Rome and came back, and the thing that sent him out of Chupka with a stolen chest and a widow's trade name has now been as far as it goes, and there is nothing further west that would teach him anything.

Which leaves the other direction. I wrote at the start of this chapter that I would write down what I saw and not what I guessed, and I am going to break that rule once, at the end, on the last page of the volume, and then stop.

At the end of the third month they take the road north to Brasso and then the long way round the Balkan country, and then south, into the hills where the two great roads cross, at Debar and Kičevo, in the Macedonian country, and he has not been in that country since he went through it as a pilgrim on somebody else's road in 879. He does not know that yet. It is not in the paymaster's book as anything but a place on a route. But I have watched this company for fourteen years and I know what it looks like when it turns, and I am telling you it has turned.

A man who has made no land and holds no land and can put sixteen hundred men in a field is a thing that lords have begun to be afraid of, and the fear of him is the only property he owns. That is where this volume ends. The next one begins where he finds out what the fear is worth, and to whom, and at what price, and I expect I shall be there with the anvil, since nobody else in this company can shoe a mule.

Notes

  1. the badge A pilgrim badge: cast lead, the size of a thumbnail, bought at the door of the shrine and pinned to the hat or the cloak, so that a man who came home from Rome could prove it. They were made by the thousand and sold for pennies and the men who sold them were not cheating anybody. It is the cheapest object in this book and the only one that was made specifically to be evidence. ↩
  2. the house of all the gods The round church in the vegetable market is the Pantheon, built by Agrippa and rebuilt by Hadrian, given to the martyrs by a pope in 609 and called Santa Maria ad Martyres ever since. The name of the man who paid for it is still cut into the front of it in letters a foot high. There is no better building in the world for a Hellene to preach outside of, and the book did not invent that. ↩
  3. the wrath of Zeus The record says only that he preached it, in the third month of 882, in Rome. What he said is the book's, and it is built on the oldest part of the Greek idea of Zeus: the god of the oath and of the guest, whose anger is not weather but debt. A Hellene of the twelfth century would have argued it in the schools. A merchant's son argued it in a market, which is why it is short. ↩
  4. Hades The brother who took the third share when the world was divided: the sky to Zeus, the sea to Poseidon, and what is under the ground to him. The helmet is the cap of invisibility that the Cyclopes made, and it is why statues of him have no face to look at. He is not the devil of the later story; he is an owner. A man who buys a faceless god and does not take him out of the chest is telling you something and does not want to be asked what. ↩
  5. the war that was lost A war is not the sum of its battles. The company won at Severin in the seventh month of 881 and destroyed eleven hundred and seventy-two men in two afternoons, and the war ended in the fifth month of 882 with the county in the hands of the man it had beaten, because a war is won by whoever is still standing in the place when the season turns. Domoszló the Brute brought twenty-seven hundred men against two hundred and twenty-two and there was no battle in it worth the name. That is how most wars end. ↩
